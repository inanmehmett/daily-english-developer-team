## Two Cups

Kimlik 916 · A1 · 217 kelime · 3 dk · başlangıç paketi

The man who cleans our stairs comes at six.

I make my tea at six too.

He has a blue bucket.

This morning I looked at him again. His shoes were wet. The rain was still on them.

I went to the kitchen. I put water in the pot. Two glasses this time.

I opened the door and stood on the stairs.

"Tea?" I said.

"Later," he said.

"It is hot now."

He looked up at the stairs above him. They were still dirty.

"Later," he said again.

I stood there with the two glasses. My hands were hot.

He looked at my hands. "Okay."

"Sugar?"

"No sugar."

He put the bucket down. He took the glass.

"The rain is bad today," I said.

"Very bad."

"Your shoes are wet."

"They are old shoes," he said.

Outside, the sea was grey and flat.

We drank our tea on the stairs. Nobody talked. A door opened downstairs and closed again.

He gave me the empty glass. "Good tea," he said. Then he took his bucket and went down.

I washed the two glasses. My bus was gone.

I put them on the table, next to each other.

Tomorrow I will make tea at six. I will make two glasses.

Then I took my bag and went to work.

## Fish on Friday

On Friday the small fish are at the front.

I go to the market on Fridays. I do not always buy fish. I like the noise and the water on the stones.

The woman in front of me comes every Friday too. She has an old bag. She always asks for the small ones.

"How much are these?" she said.

"Cheap today," the fish man said.

"Give me two."

He put two small fish on the paper. Then he looked at her bag. He put a third fish on the paper.

"I said two."

"This one is broken," he said. "Nobody buys it."

The fish was not broken. I was close and I saw it.

She looked at the fish for a moment. Then she opened her bag.

"Thank you," she said.

"Friday," he said. That was all.

"Next week?" she said.

"Next week."

She went out through the wet part of the market. Her bag was heavy on one side.

I looked at the fish man again. He was washing his hands in the cold water. He did not look at me.

"The small ones," I said.

"Two?"

"Two."

He gave me two and no third one. My bag was not old.

Outside the rain had stopped. The street was wet and bright.

I walked to the bus with my fish.

## Ten Steps

Kimlik 928 · A1 · 235 kelime · 3 dk · günlük havuz

The sea was cold, and Jonas said ten steps.

Lena stood on the stones with the towel. It was early. Nobody else was on the beach.

"Ten," Jonas said. "Not nine."

"Okay."

"Hold the towel."

"I have it."

He put one foot in the water. Then the other foot.

"One," he said. "Two."

The water came to his knees. He stopped.

"Three," Lena said.

"I know."

"Is it cold?"

"No."

He walked. Four. Five. The water came higher.

At six he could not feel his feet. That was worse.

"Six," he said. His voice was small.

Seven. The water was high now. It moved up and down.

"You can come out," Lena said.

"Ten," he said.

Lena put the towel down. She walked to the edge of the water.

Eight. Nine. He put his hands under the water and pushed.

Ten.

He turned and walked to the stones. He was slow.

Lena picked the towel up and gave it to him. He sat down.

His hands were shaking. He did not look at them.

"Ten steps," Lena said.

"Yes. Ten."

"Your lips are blue."

"I know."

The sun came over the hill. The water went white in one line.

His feet were red and hot now.

He put the towel on his head. His hands stopped shaking.

Next week the sea would be colder. He knew that.

He had said ten. He had done ten.

## Eleven Minutes

Kimlik 929 · A1 · 256 kelime · 3 dk · günlük havuz

Her bag was heavy and the bus was not coming.

The board at the stop said eleven minutes. A woman sat under the board with a shopping bag.

"Eleven," the woman said. "It has said that for a long time."

"I know this one," Marta said. "It comes."

"Then sit down."

"You will not get there faster."

"I will."

Marta looked up the road. The shop was at the top of the hill. She could walk it before the bus came.

She looked at the board again. Eleven minutes.

"I will walk," she said.

"With that bag?"

"It is not heavy."

The bag was heavy. She put it on her other arm. She started up the hill.

The first street was flat. The second street was not. Her coat was too warm. She could not open it, because of the bag.

At the third street she moved the bag again. Her hand was red where the handle was.

She counted the streets. Four more. Then three.

The bus passed her at the school. The woman with the bag was not at the window.

Then it stopped at the school gate. It stopped again at the lights.

Marta did not stop. Her legs were hot and her ears were cold.

She got to the shop. The bus was still at the corner.

She put the bag on the ground at the door. Her hand had a red line on it.

The bus came and opened its doors. Nobody got out.

She had walked it in eleven minutes.

## One Hundred

Kimlik 956 · A1 · 189 kelime · 1 dk

The rope was red and white. Nina said one hundred.

Petra sat on the step and counted. That was the rule. You do not count for yourself.

Nina's best was sixty-one. It was sixty-one for a long time.

"One hundred," Nina said. "Not ninety."

"Okay," Petra said. "Go."

The rope went round. Ten. Twenty. It was easy.

At thirty her arms were tired. Arms first. Then legs. She knew that.

"Forty," Petra said.

The rope hit the ground. Slap. Slap. Slap.

At fifty Nina's face was red. At sixty her legs were heavy.

"Sixty-one," Petra said, and stood up.

Sixty-two. New. Every jump now was new.

Seventy. Her arms were not her arms now.

Eighty. Petra counted louder.

Ninety. The street had one sound. The rope.

Ninety-five. Ninety-eight. Ninety-nine.

One hundred.

Nina stopped. She sat down on the road. She held the rope with two hands. Her breath was loud, in and out, in and out.

"One hundred," Petra said.

"Yes," Nina said. "Now you."

Petra took the rope. She stood in the road. She swung the rope once, slowly, to feel it.

"One hundred and one," Petra said.

## The London Eye

Kimlik 1035 · A1 · 132 kelime · 2 dk

The London Eye is a big wheel by the river in London. It is very slow. One turn takes thirty minutes.

Why so slow?

Here is the surprise: the wheel does not stop. People get on while it is moving. It moves so slowly that you can just walk in. Grandmothers walk in. Small children walk in. Nobody runs.

You stand in a glass capsule with about twenty people. The capsule goes up very slowly, and London opens under your feet: the river, the bridges, Big Ben. On a clear day you can see for forty kilometres.

At the top, the capsule is 135 metres high. That is taller than Big Ben.

Then you come down slowly, the door opens, and you walk out — and the wheel still does not stop.

## Thirty-One Mornings

Kimlik 1083 · A1 · 254 kelime · 2 dk · başlangıç paketi

The fox is always at the big rock. Every morning, at seven, Emma walks in the forest, and the fox is there. Day one: it sees her and runs. Day two: it runs. Day three: it runs, but late.

Emma starts to count steps. Day seven, she stops at forty steps from the rock. The fox looks at her. It does not run. Forty steps is fine. Thirty-nine is not. She tries. It runs.

She walks the same path every day. Same shoes, same hour, same slow walk. She learns the fox's rules. No fast hands. No looking in its eyes for a long time. Stand still, look at the trees, be boring. Emma is very good at being boring now.

Day fifteen: thirty steps. Day twenty-two: twenty steps. The fox eats something small in the grass and watches her with one eye. Rain comes one morning; she goes anyway. The fox is under the rock, dry and orange, and she stands in the rain, wet and stupid, and laughs very quietly. The fox does not run from a quiet laugh. Now she knows that too.

Day thirty-one. Ten steps. The fox sits. Emma sits too, on the wet grass, very slowly. The forest makes its morning sounds. Birds up high. Water somewhere. The fox closes its eyes for one second — one whole second — with Emma there, ten steps away.

Then it stands, walks along the rock, and is gone between the trees.

Emma stays on the grass. Tomorrow: nine steps. Maybe.

## The Bench

The final starts at ten. At five to ten, the coach reads the names. Tom's name is not there.

Tom sits on the bench. His boots are clean. Everyone else's boots are dirty. His father stands behind the fence with the other parents. He lifts his hand. Tom lifts his hand back. Then he looks at the grass.

The Eagles play in red and white. Marco plays in Tom's place today. Marco is fast, everyone knows it. The other team scores first. Then Marco falls and holds his leg. The coach looks at the bench. He looks at Tom for one second — then he sends Ben.

"Warm up," the coach says at half time. "Maybe."

Tom runs up and down the line. He runs harder than anyone in the game. Minute fifty, still 0-1. Minute sixty, Ben is tired. The coach calls: "Tom."

The grass is soft under his boots. The ball comes to him one time, two times. He is not fast like Marco, but he is there, again and again. Minute seventy, a corner. The ball flies in high, touches a head, falls — and Tom's foot is the first foot. 1-1.

The game ends 1-1. There are penalties now. The coach picks five names, and this time Tom's name is there, last of the five. Tom looks at the fence. His father's hands are on the fence, white.

Four penalties for them, four for us. Tom puts the ball on the spot and takes three steps back.

## The Red Shirt

Kimlik 910 · A1 · 318 kelime · 4 dk · dizi: Game Day · sezon 1 · bölüm 4

The bus was full of blue.

Blue shirts, blue hats, blue scarves. Everybody was singing. The windows were wet with breath.

Tomas got on at Market Street. His shirt was red.

He was eleven. He was new in this city. His father worked on Saturdays, so Tomas was going to the match alone, with a ticket in his pocket and a red shirt on his back — the shirt of the other team. His old team. His city before this city.

The singing stopped a little.

A very big man near the door looked at Tomas. He had a blue hat and a blue scarf and a big grey beard.

"Red," the man said.

"Yes," said Tomas. His voice was small.

"On this bus."

"Yes."

The bus went over a bridge. Nobody sang.

Then the big man smiled.

"Good," he said. "Good! A boy with his own shirt." He turned to the bus. "Look at this! Eleven years old and no fear!"

Somebody laughed. Somebody else clapped, once.

"Where are you from?" the man asked.

"Vellin. We moved in June."

"Vellin!" The man made a face like bad milk. "Terrible team. Terrible city. Wonderful bread." He moved his bag. "Sit down. You can't stand in a red shirt, it's dangerous."

Tomas sat down.

For twenty minutes the man told him about the stadium: which gate was fast, which food was bad, where the wind came in, and the name of every player, and what was wrong with every player.

At the stadium, everybody got off. The man put on his glasses and looked at Tomas's ticket.

"Block C," he said. "That's the away end. Other side. Go left at the shop, then up." He nodded. "Sing loud. Your team will lose."

"Maybe," said Tomas.

They lost, 3–0.

Tomas sang anyway, in his red shirt, with two hundred other red shirts, and it was the best Saturday since June.

## Box Number Twelve

Kimlik 829 · A1 · 390 kelime · 4 dk · dizi: New in Town · sezon 1 · bölüm 1

*Two days before. Her mother's house.*

Her mother came up from the cellar with a box.

It was old. The tape was brown and dry. On the side there was one word: HOME.

"Take this one too," her mother said.

"I don't want it."

"I know."

Maya looked at the word on the box. She knew the writing. She did not say anything about it.

"Put it in the van," her mother said.

"Mama."

"Put it in the van, and open it when you want. Or don't open it." Her mother put the box on the table. "But it goes with you."

---

There were twelve boxes. Eleven had names.

KITCHEN. BOOKS. WINTER. SHOES. Maya read the words and moved the boxes, one by one. The new flat was big and empty. Her steps were loud.

Box number twelve was different. It was old, and the tape was old too. On the side, one word: HOME.

Maya looked at it for a long time. Then she put it in the corner, behind the door.

Her phone rang. It was her mother.

"How is the new flat?"

"Good. Big."

"Did you eat something?"

"Yes, Mama."

The pizza box on the floor was empty. It was true, almost.

"And the town? Is it nice?"

"I don't know it yet," Maya said. "It's fine."

"Did you open the old box?"

"Good night, Mama."

Maya sat on a box and listened to the new flat. The water in the pipes. The wind at the window. And music. Very quiet music, from the other side of the wall. Old songs. Somebody was there, with a radio.

She went to the window. Outside, the street was dark. But there were lights.

The street lamp at the bus stop. A small shop at the corner, with warm yellow light. And on the wall across the street — two green eyes. A grey cat sat there and looked at her.

"Hello," Maya said to the window. The cat did not move.

Three lights, Maya thought. A lamp, a shop, a cat.

She made her bed between the boxes. The music stopped on the other side of the wall. The flat was quiet again.

Tomorrow I will open the boxes, she thought.

Box number twelve waited in the corner, behind the door.

Not that one, Maya thought. Not yet.

## The Wrong Key

*The same morning, 6:15. Number six.*

Ben got up at a quarter past six, because he always did.

He put the radio on, very quiet. Old songs. Then he made tea.

He took two cups out of the cupboard. He looked at them for a moment. Then he put one cup back.

He drank his tea at the table and read yesterday's newspaper again. At seven he heard steps in flat number five. He smiled at the wall. That flat was empty for eleven months.

"Boxes," he said. "Somebody with boxes."

Then he got his shopping bag. He made a list of four things. He went out early, much too early for the shop.

---

The door closed behind her. Click.

Maya looked at the key in her hand. It was small and silver. The mailbox key.

The flat key was inside. On the kitchen table. Next to the bread list.

"No," Maya said to the door. "No, no, no."

She was in her slippers. It was eight in the morning. She knew nobody in this town.

Steps came up the stairs. A man with a shopping bag. He was maybe sixty, with a friendly red face.

"Good morning!" he said. "You are number five! You are the new neighbour. I am Ben, number six. You know what? I heard your boxes on Monday. Boxes here, boxes there. Moving is hard work. I moved here thirty years ago. My radio—"

"Sorry—" Maya said quietly. "I... my key is inside."

Ben stopped. He looked at her slippers. He looked at the small silver key in her hand.

"Ah," he said. "The wrong key. It happens to everybody."

"It's fine," Maya said. It was not fine.

"You know what? Mrs Rossi on the first floor has all the extra keys. She drinks her tea at eight. Perfect time. Come with me."

Maya came with him. Ben talked all the way down. About the building. About the postman years. About the radio songs in the morning. "You hear them? I can make it quiet!"

"No," Maya said. "It's... nice."

Mrs Rossi opened her door with a big cup of tea. Five minutes later, Maya had the extra key in her hand.

"Thank you," she said to Mrs Rossi.

At her door, Ben smiled. "Welcome to Garden Street, number five."

He went into his flat. The radio started again on the other side of the wall. Old songs, quiet and warm.

Maya stood alone in front of her open door.

"Thank you, Ben," she said. But she said it very quietly, and only to the wall.

## Milk and Bread

Kimlik 831 · A1 · 427 kelime · 4 dk · dizi: New in Town · sezon 1 · bölüm 3

*Wednesday, 5:40 in the morning. The back of the shop.*

Mr Costa opened the shop at six, but he came at half past five.

First the bread. Then the oranges. Then the notebook.

The notebook was small and old. He put it on the box of oranges. It was the same box every morning. Then he read yesterday's page.

*Mrs Rossi — tea, biscuits. Ben, number six — four things, always four. New girl, number five — milk, bread, eggs. Then milk and bread.*

He looked at the last line for a moment.

"Rain today," he said, to the oranges.

Then he took one bottle of milk and one bread. He put them on the counter, next to the till. He wrote nothing in the notebook. He did not have a name for that page yet.

---

The small shop smelled like bread and oranges.

Maya took a basket. Milk, bread, eggs, tea. The man behind the counter was maybe sixty. He had grey hair and slow, careful hands.

"Good evening," Maya said.

"Good evening. Bag?"

"No, thank you."

"Good, good."

That was all. Maya paid and went home. But the light of the shop stayed warm behind her. She walked in that light all the way down the street.

On Tuesday she came again. Milk and bread.

"No eggs today?" the man asked.

Maya looked up. He remembered.

"No... just milk and bread. Thank you."

"Good, good."

On Wednesday it rained. Maya ran into the shop with wet hair. On the counter, next to the till, stood one bottle of milk and one bread.

"For me?" Maya asked.

"You come at six," the man said. "It is six."

Maya paid. Her face was warm. At the door, she stopped.

"My name is Maya," she said. "Number five, Garden Street."

The man took the small notebook from under the counter. He opened a new page. He wrote slowly, with his careful hands.

"Maya," he said. "Milk and bread. I am Mr Costa."

"Thank you, Mr Costa."

"New page for new people," he said. "Good, good."

Outside, the rain was small and soft now. On the step of the shop sat the grey cat from the wall. It looked at Maya's bag. It looked at Maya.

"This is my milk," Maya told the cat.

The cat followed her. It followed her down the street, past the bus stop, to the door of number five. Then it sat down and waited.

"You don't live here," Maya said.

The cat looked at her with green eyes and did not agree.

## The Cat at the Door

Kimlik 832 · A1 · 435 kelime · 4 dk · dizi: New in Town · sezon 1 · bölüm 4

*Two winters ago. Garden Street, in the snow.*

The cat came down the street in January, and it was very thin.

Mrs Rossi saw it first. She put out some fish on a saucer, by her door. "Poor thing," she said.

Then Ben saw it. He put out milk in a small bowl. He talked to it for ten minutes. The cat listened and did not answer.

Then Mr Costa saw it, at the shop door in the evening. He put out food on a piece of paper.

"You have a home?" he asked the cat.

The cat ate the food and looked up the dark street.

Mr Costa went inside and opened his notebook. He did not write the cat down. The cat was not a customer, and it had no name.

Three people fed it that week. Nobody said a name out loud. And the cat stayed.

---

In the morning, the cat was at the door.

It sat on the step of number five and looked up.

"Good morning," Maya said. "You are not my cat."

The cat did not agree. It did not leave.

Maya walked to the shop. The cat walked with her. At the shop door it sat down and waited. It knew the rules.

Inside, Maya took milk and bread. Then she stood in front of the cat food for a long time. Small cans with pictures of happy cats.

She took one can. Then two.

At the counter, Mr Costa looked at the cans. He looked at Maya.

"The grey cat," Maya said. "It waits at my door. It's not my cat."

"Good, good," said Mr Costa.

He opened his small notebook. He found her page and wrote slowly.

"Maya," he read. "Milk, bread, cat."

"It's NOT my cat," Maya said again.

"Good, good," said Mr Costa.

At home, Maya put the food on an old plate. She put the plate on the step, outside.

"Outside," she told the cat. "You live outside."

The cat ate everything. Then it washed its face and sat next to the empty plate, like a small grey king.

In the evening, Maya asked Ben about the cat.

"That cat? Nobody's cat," Ben said. "It came two winters ago. You know what? Everybody feeds it. Mrs Rossi, Mr Costa, me. Everybody feeds it — but nobody gives it a name."

"Why not?"

"A name is a big thing," Ben said. "A name means: you are mine."

That night, Maya looked out of the window. The cat sat on the wall, in its place.

"Good night, cat," she said. Just cat. For now.

## Bus Number 7

Kimlik 833 · A1 · 487 kelime · 4 dk · dizi: New in Town · sezon 1 · bölüm 5

*The same morning, 8:20. The landing.*

Ben wrote the note at eight o'clock, on the back of his shopping list.

*Number 7 from OUR side goes out of town. Number 7 from the OTHER side goes to the centre. Ben, number six.*

He read it twice. Then he went out to the landing and stood in front of number five.

The flat was quiet. Maybe she was asleep. Maybe she was in the shower. He lifted his hand to knock. Then he did not knock. Eight in the morning is early for a new neighbour. And a man who talks a lot knows when he is too much.

He looked at the gap under her door. The note would go under it easily.

"She has a paper," he said to himself. "She has the times. She is a young woman, not a child."

He put the note in his pocket and went back inside, and the radio came on, quiet.

---

Maya stood at the bus stop with a paper in her hand. The bus stop was under the street lamp.

City office, 10 o'clock. New town, new papers. Her first time in the centre.

The bus came. Number 7. Maya got on and sat by the window.

The bus went past the shop. Past the school. Past houses and more houses. Then past fields.

Fields?

Maya looked at the paper. She looked out of the window. There were cows.

"Excuse me," she said to the woman next to her. "Is this the bus to the centre?"

"Oh no, dear," the woman said. "This 7 goes out. You want the 7 from the other side of the street."

Two number 7s. One street. Maya closed her eyes.

"It's fine," she said. It was 9:40, and she was late.

"It happens to everybody," the woman said. "Get off with me at the big tree. We cross the road together, and the other 7 comes at ten."

They crossed at the big tree, in the middle of nowhere. The woman had two heavy bags. Maya carried one. They stood under the tree and waited.

"New here?" the woman asked.

"Three weeks. Garden Street."

"Ah, Garden Street! Then you know Ben. Everybody knows Ben."

Maya smiled. "He talks a lot."

"He does," the woman laughed. "Good man. He talks for two, because his flat is quiet for one."

The other 7 came at ten. The woman rode with her all the way and showed her the door of the city office.

"There, dear. You are only twenty minutes late, dear. In this town, twenty minutes is on time."

The office was easy. The papers were easy. The way home was easy — one bus, right side of the street.

That evening, Maya told the cat everything.

"The town is big," she said. "But it is one street, and then another street. And streets have ends."

The cat agreed, this time.

## A Name on the Mailbox

Kimlik 834 · A1 · 410 kelime · 4 dk · dizi: New in Town · sezon 1 · bölüm 6

*Years before. A cellar.*

The box was full: a blanket, three books, a brown radio, one photograph.

The hands that packed it were slow. They put the photograph on the top, face up. Then they took it out again. They put it under the books, face down.

Then the tape. Old tape, from the shelf. The new tape was in the kitchen, and the kitchen was full of people that day.

Then the pen.

The hand wrote one word on the side of the box, in tall thin letters: HOME.

It was not a label. There were no other words on it — no *kitchen*, no *books*, no *winter*. Just the one.

The box went under the stairs, and the light went off, and for a long time nobody moved it.

---

There were six mailboxes in the hall.

ROSSI. BEN COLE. ADAMS. WEBER. KAYA. And one empty window: number five.

Maya stood in front of it with a small white card and a pen.

It was only a name on a card. But her hand waited.

A name on a mailbox means: I live here. Letters can come here. I am not a guest.

Maya wrote slowly: MAYA. Her letters were small and round. She pushed the card into the little window. It fit perfectly.

The front door opened. Ben came in with his shopping bag.

"Oh!" he said. He came close and read the card like a newspaper. "MAYA. Number five. You know what? Now you exist."

"Ben, I lived here before the card."

"Not for the postman," Ben said. "Thirty years I carried letters. No name, no person. A name on the box, and the whole world can find you." He smiled and went up the stairs. "Welcome again, number five!"

Maya looked at the card. Six boxes, six names. Hers was the newest and the whitest.

Upstairs, in the flat, she made tea. The evening sun came in. And then she saw it.

Box number twelve, in the corner, behind the door. The word on its side: HOME.

Maya went closer with her cup.

The letters of HOME were tall and thin. Her letters were small and round. It was not her writing.

She knew whose writing it was. She did not say the name, not even quietly.

She touched the old tape with one finger.

"Not yet," she said.

The box waited. It was good at waiting. It had waited for years.

## Rain and an Umbrella

Kimlik 835 · A1 · 434 kelime · 4 dk · dizi: New in Town · sezon 1 · bölüm 7

*Nine years ago. The same building.*

Ben bought two umbrellas on the same day, in the shop by the station.

"Two?" the girl at the till said.

"Two," Ben said. "One for me. One for somebody else."

"Your wife?"

"No," Ben said. "Just somebody else. There is always somebody with no umbrella."

He walked home in the rain under the first one, and he put the second one behind his door, and it stayed there.

Some years it came out. A boy from the school, once. Mrs Rossi's sister. A man at the bus stop who never brought it back. That was fine. That is the point.

But the second umbrella always came home again. Or a new second umbrella took its place. Ben did not think about this very much. He only knew that a man with one umbrella is poor.

---

The rain started fast and cold.

Maya stood at the bus stop with bread in her bag and no coat. The water ran down her neck.

"Number five!" a voice called.

Ben came down the street under a big black umbrella. He walked like the rain was nothing.

"Come, come. You know what? I smelled this rain at lunch. My knee is better than the radio weather man."

They walked home together, close under the big umbrella. Ben talked about rain, about the postman years — "letters must stay dry, people can get wet" — and about soup.

At her door, he put the umbrella in her hand.

"No," Maya said. "It's yours. You need—"

"I have two," Ben said. "A man with one umbrella is poor. Take it, take it. Umbrellas like walking."

The next day the sun came back. Maya stood in front of Ben's door with the umbrella and a small bag of biscuits from Mr Costa's shop.

She knocked.

The door opened a little. Behind Ben, Maya saw the flat for one second. Very clean. Very quiet. One chair by the radio. One cup on the table.

One chair.

"The umbrella," Maya said. "And... these. Now we are even."

Ben looked at the biscuits. Then he laughed — a big, warm laugh.

"Even? Neighbours are never even, Maya. That is the good part." He took one biscuit and gave back the bag. "The umbrella stays with you. Give it to the next wet person. That is how umbrellas work."

The door closed. Behind it, the radio played its old songs.

Maya stood in the hall with the umbrella, the biscuits, and something new and warm in her chest.

Never even, she thought. What a strange, nice rule.

## The Neighbour's Radio

Kimlik 836 · A1 · 444 kelime · 4 dk · dizi: New in Town · sezon 1 · bölüm 8

*Monday, 6:15. Number six.*

Ben got up at a quarter past six, because he always did.

He stood by the bed for a moment. His head was hot and his arms were heavy. The kitchen was six steps away and it looked further.

He did not put the radio on. His hand was on the button, and then it was not.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Tomorrow it will be better."

He drank some water from the tap. He never did that. Then he went back to bed with his socks on, and he never did that either.

At eight o'clock he heard steps in flat number five. Her kettle. Her door.

Ben lay in the quiet and listened to his neighbour making tea. He did not knock on her wall. A man does not knock on a wall for a cold.

---

On Monday morning, the wall was quiet.

No radio. No old songs. Maya drank her tea and listened. Nothing.

Maybe he sleeps late, she thought.

On Tuesday morning the wall was still quiet. The quiet was loud now. It filled the whole kitchen.

Maya thought about it at work. She thought about it on the bus. One chair. One cup. A man who talks for two.

In the evening she bought bread and lemons at Mr Costa's.

"For Ben," she said. "His radio is quiet."

Mr Costa stopped. He put one orange in the bag, free.

"Good, good," he said. But his eyes said: go quickly.

Maya stood in front of Ben's door. Her heart was loud. She knocked.

Steps. Slow steps. The door opened.

Ben stood there in a thick sweater, with a red nose and tired eyes.

"Number five," he said. His voice was small; half of his voice was gone. "A cold. It's nothing."

"It's not nothing," Maya said. "You sound like a quiet radio."

Ben laughed, and the laugh became a cough.

Maya came in. She made tea with lemon. She put the bread and the orange on the table. She turned the radio on, and the old songs came back, quiet and warm.

Ben sat in his one chair and drank his tea. For the first time, he was the quiet one.

"You know what?" he said finally. "The flat is very quiet without the radio."

"I know," Maya said. "I hear your songs through the wall. Every morning."

Ben looked at her. "They don't wake you?"

"They tell me you are there," Maya said. "It's a good wall."

Ben was quiet for a moment. His eyes were shiny; maybe from the cold.

The next morning, the songs came through the wall again.

A little louder than before.

## Saturday Market

*Six weeks ago. Her first Saturday.*

Maya came to the square at ten and stopped at the edge of the market.

It was very full. Bags, voices, a man shouting about fish. Two women stood in front of the bread and talked for a long time. Nobody was in a hurry. Everybody knew somebody.

She had no list. She had a bag and eleven pounds.

Maya stood there for four minutes. Then she walked back up the hill with her empty bag. She bought bread at Mr Costa's shop instead. In that shop there was one man, one counter and no crowd.

At home she said to herself: next Saturday.

She said it again the Saturday after that.

---

The Saturday market filled the square.

Bread, fish, apples, cheese, flowers. People everywhere, bags everywhere, voices everywhere.

Today Maya had a list and a bag.

"Number five!" Ben stood at the cheese stand. "This is Maya, from Garden Street," he told the cheese man, the fish woman, and two old ladies. "Number five!"

"Hello, Maya," said the cheese man, like they were old friends.

Mrs Rossi waved from the flower stand with a big cup of tea; the market had tea for her too, everywhere.

At the fruit stand, Maya found oranges. The orange man said: "You are the one from Mr Costa's street! He says: milk, bread, cat."

Maya laughed. "The cat is not my cat."

"Of course," the orange man said, and gave her one extra orange.

The grey cat sat under the fish stand, working.

Maya bought everything on her list. Then she stopped at the flower stand. Small yellow flowers, in little pots. They were not on the list.

For the window, she thought. A flat with flowers in the window is not a guest's flat.

She bought two pots.

On the way home, the cat came out from under the fish stand. It did not walk behind Maya today.

It walked next to her.

Bag, flowers, cat — down Garden Street, past the bus stop, home.

The market noise was far behind them now. It was a good noise, Maya thought. It was noise with names.

At the door of number five, the cat sat down and looked up.

"Not yet," Maya said. But she said it slowly this time.

## Home

Kimlik 838 · A1 · 445 kelime · 4 dk · dizi: New in Town · sezon 1 · bölüm 10

*Seven years ago. The garden.*

The bicycle was too big for her, and that was the point: she would grow.

Her father held the saddle and ran three steps and let go. Maya went four metres. She stopped with both feet on the ground and shouted at him.

"Again!"

"Again," he said.

Somebody took a photograph from the kitchen door. In the photograph he is laughing. He is not looking at the camera. His hand is still in the air behind the bicycle.

Through the kitchen window you can see a small brown radio on the windowsill, playing to nobody.

"Ten more minutes," her mother called from the door. "Then dinner."

"Ten more minutes," her father said, and they took twenty.

---

On Sunday evening, the wind came from the sea.

The grey cat sat at the door of number five. It made itself small against the cold.

Maya opened the door with her rubbish bag in her hand. She looked at the cat. The cat looked at her.

The wind pushed cold air into the hall.

"Oh, come in," Maya said. "Just for tonight."

The cat walked in like it had lived there for years. It smelled the kitchen. It smelled the boxes. Then it sat down in the warmest corner and closed its eyes.

Maya made tea and watched it.

"You need a name," she said. "Everybody feeds you, but nobody names you. A name means: you are mine."

The cat opened one eye.

"Twelve," Maya said. "Your name is Twelve. Like the box. Good things wait."

Twelve closed the eye. Agreed.

And then, because the flat was warm, and the cat was sleeping, and the yellow flowers stood in the window — Maya went to the corner behind the door.

She picked up box number twelve and put it on the kitchen table. She touched the tall, thin letters of HOME.

"Okay," she said. "Now."

The old tape came off slowly. Inside: a winter blanket that smelled like the old house. Three books. A small brown radio, her grandfather's radio, older than Maya.

And at the bottom, face down, a photo.

A garden. A very small Maya on a bicycle. And behind her, holding the bicycle, a man, laughing.

Maya sat down slowly.

"Hello, Papa," she said quietly.

The cat came and sat next to her, warm against her leg. Through the wall, Ben's radio played its old songs. Outside, Mr Costa's shop light was on at the corner.

Maya put the photo on the windowsill, next to the flowers. She put the little brown radio next to it.

"Okay," she said to the flat, to the cat, to the street.

"Home."

## The Wrong Platform

Kimlik 895 · A1 · 270 kelime · 3 dk · dizi: Passport · sezon 1 · bölüm 4

The train left at 9:40. Nora was on it. That was the problem.

Platform 3, the ticket said. Nora stood on Platform 3. But this station had a Platform 3 and a Platform 3A, and the 9:40 to the city left from 3A.

Nora understood this after twenty minutes. Her train went past small houses, then fields, then a river. The city was behind her.

"Excuse me," she asked the conductor. "This train goes to the city?"

"Other direction," he said. "This one goes to Marden."

"What is in Marden?"

The conductor thought about it. "Marden," he said.

Nora got off at Marden. It was a small station with flowers in old tea pots. The next train back: two hours.

Two hours in a town with one street.

She walked the street. A bakery. A bookshop. A river with a green bridge. On the bridge, an old man fed the birds and said good morning to her — just like that, to a stranger.

In the bakery, the woman gave her warm bread and asked her name. In the bookshop, a fat cat slept on the maps.

Nora sat by the river with the bread. The water was slow. Her phone had three messages from work. She read them later — much later.

The train back came at 12:10. From the window, Nora looked at the green bridge, the one street, the flowers in the tea pots.

In the city, at the big station, people ran everywhere. Nora walked slowly.

She kept the wrong ticket in her coat pocket.

Some mistakes you throw away. This one she wanted to keep.

## The Bird on the Balcony

Kimlik 899 · A1 · 288 kelime · 3 dk · dizi: Wild · sezon 1 · bölüm 3

The bird came every morning at seven.

It was small and brown, with quick black eyes. It sat on the balcony rail and waited. Samuel put out bread and one piece of apple. The bird ate, looked at him, and flew away.

Samuel was seventy-eight. He got up at six, made his tea, and cut the apple. The bird was his first appointment of the day. Some days, it was the only one.

"You are late," he told it on rainy days. It was never late.

On Tuesday, the balcony was empty at seven. At eight, it was still empty. The bread got hard. The apple got brown.

Samuel stood at the window for a long time.

"It's a bird," he said to the kitchen. "Birds come and go."

But he left the bread out. At lunch, he cut a new piece of apple. Old bread does not welcome anybody.

On Wednesday: nothing. Samuel read his newspaper on the balcony, in the cold, from seven to nine.

On Thursday morning, there was a sound. Not one bird — many. Samuel opened the door.

The little brown bird sat on the rail. And next to it, small and round and afraid, sat three young birds. Their feathers were new. Their eyes were quick and black, like hers.

"Ah," said Samuel. "You were busy."

He went to the kitchen and came back with bread and one whole apple.

The young birds ate everything. Their mother sat on the rail and watched them, tired and still.

Samuel drank his tea next to the open door.

"Tomorrow at seven," he said. "All of you. Table for four."

The bird looked at him with her quick black eyes. It was almost a yes.

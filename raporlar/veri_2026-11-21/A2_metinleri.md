## No Phone for a Day

Kimlik 137 · A2 · 138 kelime · 3 dk

Yesterday I decided to spend a whole day without my phone. It was harder than I expected. I usually check my phone about 150 times per day, but I didn't realize this until it wasn't there. At first, I kept reaching for my pocket automatically. My hand would go there every few minutes, searching for the familiar shape. I felt disconnected from the world. What if someone needed to contact me urgently? What if I missed important news? But as the hours passed, something interesting happened. I started noticing things I normally miss. I heard birds singing outside my window. I actually tasted my food instead of scrolling while eating. I had a real conversation with my neighbor instead of just nodding while looking at my screen. By evening, I felt more relaxed than I had in months.

## The Neighbour's Pot

"I washed it twice."

"It is a heavy pot," she said.

Then I ate at the table, from the pot. The lid was next to my plate. It was very good. I did not think about my mother's kitchen, and then I did.

I washed the pot after. I did not wash it twice.

I put it back in the cupboard, at the front. I can reach it there. Next week she will need it again.

Then I put the bag of oranges by the door.

## Salt

Kimlik 919 · A2 · 492 kelime · 5 dk

I was putting the pan on when somebody knocked twice.

It was the new tenant from the top floor. She had a cup in her hand and the cup was empty.

"I am sorry," she said. "Do you have salt?"

"Come in."

"I can wait here."

"The door is open and the water is on the stove. Come in."

She came in as far as the mat and stopped there. I gave her the salt from the cupboard, in the packet. She poured some into the cup. Then she looked at how much she had taken.

"That is enough," I said.

"It is a lot."

"It is salt."

She put the packet on the counter. Then she did not go. She looked at the pan, and at the window. Then at the bags of flour on the fridge.

"You cook here every day?" she said.

"Twice a day. My son eats at seven."

"Mine is at his father's this month."

She said it the way people say the number of a bus. Then she looked at the pan again.

I asked her if she had found the market. She said she had found the big one on the main road, with the trolleys. I told her about the other one, behind the mosque. The tomatoes are outside there, and on Fridays they cost less. She wrote nothing down. She only nodded twice, and stayed where she was, on the mat.

Upstairs somebody was moving boxes. We both listened to it for a moment.

"That is my brother," she said. "He is taking the empty ones."

"You have a lot of them."

"We had a big flat."

The water started to move in the pan. I put the rice in and turned the gas down. When I looked up she had taken one step off the mat. She was standing properly in the kitchen, with the cup in both hands. She looked like someone at a bus stop who has decided to sit.

I did not tell her to sit down. I put two plates on the counter. There were always two plates in the evening. Then I went on cooking. She watched the pan. She asked what was in it and I told her. She said her grandmother put butter in at the end. I said mine did too.

Then the noise upstairs stopped, and she heard it stop before I did.

"He is finished," she said. "I have to give him the keys."

"Take the packet."

"I have enough."

"Take it. You will need it again tomorrow."

She took the packet. At the door she turned round. She said thank you for the market, not for the salt.

I heard her on the stairs, then the two of them, then the outside door.

The rice needed five more minutes. I put the lid on and stood at the window until it was ready.

Tomorrow I will buy two packets of salt.

The shelf is up. Nobody in this flat can say the shelf is not up.

I had a shelf, a drill, and a free Saturday. The wall in this flat is not a normal wall.

"Viktor, please."

"Read the box."

"The box says a six."

"The box is a suggestion."

"That was the wall," he said.

"It was already loose."

"You have a shelf and no wall."

"I have two holes."

"Two holes and a hole."

"That is three."

"That is what I said."

"It is a slide."

"That is not how shelves work."

"It is how this shelf works."

## Thirty-Six Payments

The phone came in a white box with a plastic film on the screen.

Andrei bought it from the shop next to the bus station. That shop sells phones and also suitcases. The man in the shop said it was new. It was the last one of that model. The price was thirty-six instalments.

Andrei paid the first instalment at the counter. He signed his name at the bottom. Then he put the box in his bag. He did not take it out until he was home.

He kept the film on the screen.

"Take the film off," his cousin said, on the first Thursday. "It comes with the film. You are supposed to take it off."

"It protects it."

"From what?"

"From this," Andrei said, and moved his hand at the room.

"You cannot see the screen properly."

"I can see it."

For six months he kept it on. The film went a little yellow at the corners. A line of dust gathered along the top. He wiped it with his sleeve when nobody was looking. The white box stayed on top of the wardrobe. When somebody at work asked to see the phone, he held it himself. He turned it round at a distance, like a man showing a document.

His cousin came every Thursday evening. They drank tea at the table by the window. They talked about the shop, and about the man who sold it.

"Thirty more," Andrei said.

"Thirty what?"

"Instalments."

"For a phone from the suitcase shop."

"It is the same phone they sell in the mall."

"Then buy it in the mall."

"I have bought it."

That was true. It was the same model. The instalments came to the mall price and a little more. Andrei had done that sum in the shop and had not minded.

He kept the phone face down on the table when they ate. He did not put keys in the same pocket. Once he dropped it on the kitchen floor. It landed on its back. He sat down for a moment with it in both hands.

In the seventh month the film began to lift at one corner. It caught his thumb every time he used the screen.

"Take it off," his cousin said. "It is doing nothing now."

Andrei put his nail under the corner. The film came away in one piece. It hung from his fingers, yellow along the edge. The room was in the screen for the first time.

The crack went from the corner to the middle, and it was old.

## The Deposit

Kimlik 925 · A2 · 565 kelime · 5 dk

The list comes out of the landlord's pocket, folded twice.

In that building the corridor light goes on at six and goes off by itself. The bins go out on Tuesday. The lift has been broken since the winter. The notice about it is still taped to the door.

Sibel and her sister had the flat at the top. They were leaving because the rent had gone up. It had gone up in the spring too. The deposit was two months' rent. They had paid it in cash on the first day.

The flat was empty when the landlord came. The boxes were out on the landing. Sibel had washed the floor twice. Once for the dust, once for the water she spilt.

"The walls," the landlord said.

"We painted the walls."

"You painted them white."

"They were white."

"Not this white."

"White is white."

He took out the list. He read from it the way people read at a counter.

"Painting. Cleaning. The tap in the kitchen. The key to the letterbox."

"We left the keys on the counter."

"Not the letterbox key."

"It was never in the drawer."

"The tap was broken when we came."

"It is on the list."

"Which list?"

"The building list."

"I have never seen it."

"The one from before you," he said.

Sibel looked at the paper. It had six lines and no names on it.

"Cleaning for an empty flat?"

"It is a service."

"I washed the floor twice."

"Everybody washes the floor."

"How much comes back?"

He turned the list over and did the sum on the back. Then he showed her the number. It was less than half.

"This is not fair."

"This is the rule," he said. "It is the same for everybody."

"For you too?"

"I do not pay rent here."

That was true. It had been the same for the family below them. The same for the students before that. Nobody had ever asked to see the list from before. The building filled and emptied and the list stayed in the same pocket.

"Sign at the bottom."

"And if I do not sign?"

"Then it takes longer," he said.

"Longer than what?"

"Than this."

He held the pen out and waited. Sibel signed. She took the money. She put it in her coat and not in her bag, and buttoned the coat.

The landlord went down the stairs with one hand on the rail. On the floor below he said good morning to somebody. The somebody did not answer.

The boxes were still on the landing. The van was coming in the evening.

Then the door of the flat below opened. The woman who lived there came up with her keys in her hand. She looked at the boxes and she looked at Sibel. She opened her own door wide.

She carried the first box in herself. Sibel took the second one. They put them in her hall, against the wall. She moved a chair to make room for the last two.

Sibel started to say something. The woman shook her head once and went inside.

The floors in that building were thin. The woman had not asked a single question.

She came back out with tea and they sat on the stairs and waited. The corridor light went off while they sat there. Neither of them got up to press it.

## The Bowl

Kimlik 950 · A2 · 414 kelime · 2 dk

The bowl had been on my counter for six days.

It was white with a blue line round the edge, and it was empty, and that was the problem.

Mrs Ana from the second floor had sent it up when we were ill. Soup, with lemon in it. All three of us had it, even the boy, who eats nothing when he is ill. He had it twice.

I washed the bowl on the first day. Then it stood on the counter.

"Take it down," my husband said on Thursday. "She will think we lost it."

"I cannot take it down like this."

"Like what? It is clean."

"It is empty."

He looked at the bowl, and then at me, and went back to his newspaper. He grew up in a building with a lift and a doorman. He does not know about bowls.

In my grandmother's house a dish never went home empty. It could carry very little. A few biscuits. Sugar cubes. Once, I remember, three eggs in a nest of paper. But it never went home with nothing. An empty dish says the matter is closed. A full one says it is not.

The problem was what to put in it. Mrs Ana cooks better than me, and we both know it. If I made a soup, it would be a small exam, and I would fail it.

I stood in front of the cupboard for a while.

On the top shelf there was the bag from my mother. Dried mulberries, from the village, from her own tree. She sends them twice a year. You cannot buy them here. I had been keeping them, the way you keep things that cannot be bought.

I filled the bowl.

Mrs Ana opened the door with her glasses in her hand. She looked into the bowl. She took one mulberry and ate it there, standing in the door.

"Where are these from?"

"My mother dries them. From her tree."

"You cannot buy these."

"No."

She held the bowl with both hands then, a little higher than before.

"How is the boy?"

"Eating again."

"Then it was a good soup," she said, and we said good night.

A week later a plate came up. Stuffed peppers, still warm. And the plate was hers, so now the plate is the problem.

My husband says this will never end now.

I put the peppers in a dish, washed the plate, and stood in front of the cupboard.

## Wet Paint

"The second coat goes the other way."

"I tested it."

"There is a finger in the door."

"A fingerprint."

"It is deep."

"It was a strong test."

Next Sunday I am painting the kitchen door. I have a system now. Little finger, rug, open door. Hana says the kitchen door is fine.

It is fine. But now I am good at this.

## The Third Lamp

The hill starts at the bakery and ends at the school gate. There are three lamps on it. One at the bottom, one in the middle, one at the top.

Rosa has a bike. The bike is old, and the hill is the hill.

Every child in the street knows the game. You ride up. You do not stop. You do not put your foot down. The lamp where you stop is your lamp.

Rosa's lamp was the second one. It was the second one all summer.

On Sunday morning the street was empty. Luka sat on the wall by the bakery with two apples.

"Third lamp," Rosa said.

"You say it every time."

"Today I say it once."

Before the second lamp the hill gets serious. It looks the same. It is not the same.

Everything after that was new road.

Luka came up the hill on foot, with the apples.

"Third lamp," he said.

"Once," she said. "I said it once."

It was the best apple of the summer.

## Two More Floors

Kimlik 988 · A2 · 326 kelime · 2 dk

The paper on the lift said OUT OF ORDER. The paper was old. The tape was older.

Marko looked at the box, and then at the stairs. The box was water — six big bottles. Water is honest. It is exactly as heavy as it says.

Fourth floor, the order said. Sixteen steps to a floor. He did the sum: sixty-four steps.

He carried the box on his shoulder, one hand on the rail. First floor, easy. Second floor, fine. On the third floor his arm started to talk to him. On the fourth, it was shouting.

He put the box down in front of flat nine and rang the bell.

Nobody came.

His phone rang. It was the customer.

"Are you close?"

"I am at your door. Flat nine, fourth floor."

"Oh," the voice said. "I am sorry. The order is wrong. It is flat nine — but the sixth floor. The building has two nines."

Marko looked up the stairs. They turned and went on, the way stairs do.

Two more floors. Thirty-two steps.

He could say: come down, please. Some couriers say it. The rule of the company was clear: to the door. But rules were not the reason. He had carried it sixty-four steps. Sixty-four steps do not agree to stop at a wrong door.

He changed shoulders. The left one was fresh. He counted this time, out loud, quietly: one, two, three.

At sixteen, the fifth floor.

At thirty-two, the sixth.

The right flat nine had a mat and a smell of soup. A big man opened the door and saw the box and the stairs behind Marko, and understood both.

"The lift?" he said.

"Broken."

"Six floors?"

"Four," Marko said. "Then two more."

The man took the box inside like it was empty. He came back with a glass of cold water and stood there while Marko drank all of it, slowly, by the open door.

Going down was free.

## The Island Where Rabbits Won

Kimlik 1023 · A2 · 287 kelime · 2 dk · başlangıç paketi

A small island in southern Japan has about nine hundred wild rabbits and almost no people. Ferries bring visitors every day. The rabbits run to them, because visitors bring food. The island is called Ōkunoshima, and it has a second name: Rabbit Island. For thirty years, it had no name at all. It was removed from Japanese maps on purpose.

### The Island That Was Erased

In 1929, the army built a secret factory here and made poison gas. The island was chosen because it was small, quiet, and far from cities. Workers were told not to speak about their jobs. Maps of the area simply showed empty sea. The factory produced thousands of tons of gas until the end of the Second World War, and many workers became seriously ill from the chemicals.

### After the War

The factory was closed and the buildings were left standing. Their concrete walls are still there today, grey and empty, with trees growing through the windows. The island has a museum about the gas works, so visitors can learn what happened. Nobody is completely sure how the rabbits arrived. One story says schoolchildren released eight pets in 1971. Another says test animals were set free, though a former worker said all of them were killed when the factory closed.

### Rules for a Rabbit Island

The rabbits multiplied with no natural enemies on the island. Now there are so many that the local government asks visitors not to pick them up, not to chase them, and not to feed them bread or vegetables, which make them ill. ## Santorini: The Island That Exploded

Kimlik 1036 · A2 · 172 kelime · 2 dk

Every evening, thousands of people stand on a cliff in Santorini and watch the sunset. Most of them do not know one thing: they are standing on the edge of a volcano.

Santorini was a round island once. About 3,600 years ago, the volcano under it exploded. It was one of the biggest explosions in human history. The middle of the island went under the sea. What is left today is the ring — a curve of high cliffs around deep blue water.

That famous view from the white villages? You are looking into the mouth of the volcano. The water in the middle is the crater, filled by the sea.

The volcano is not dead. It is only sleeping. In the middle of the water there is a small black island. It grew out of the sea in new eruptions, and it is still warm in places.

So tomorrow evening, the tourists will come again with their cameras. The sunset is beautiful. The ground under their feet has a longer story.

## The Eiffel Tower They Wanted to Destroy

Kimlik 1037 · A2 · 174 kelime · 2 dk

The Eiffel Tower had a death date.

When it was built in 1889, the plan was simple: keep it for twenty years, then tear it down. It was never meant to be permanent. Many famous artists in Paris hated it. One writer ate lunch in the tower's restaurant every day — because, he said, it was the only place in Paris where he could not see the tower.

So why is it still standing?

Radio saved it. Around 1909, when the twenty years were ending, the tower found a new job: its top was perfect for the new radio antennas. A giant antenna was useful. Useful things do not get torn down.

The tower has one more secret: it changes size. Metal grows in the heat, so on hot summer days the tower is about fifteen centimetres taller than in winter. It also leans a tiny bit away from the sun.

Today seven million people visit it every year — a tower that exists because, at the right moment, it learned to do something.

## Alice's Adventures in Wonderland

Kimlik 1039 · A2 · 8615 kelime · 48 dk

### Bölüm 1: The Rabbit with a Watch

It was a hot afternoon and Alice was sitting on the bank with nothing to do.

Her sister was reading a book. Alice had looked into it twice. There were no pictures in it and no conversations, and Alice could not see the use of a book like that.

She was beginning to think about making a chain of daisies when a white rabbit ran past her.

That was not very strange. There were rabbits in that field.

Then the rabbit took a watch out of its waistcoat pocket, looked at it, and said, "Oh dear! Oh dear! I shall be late!" and put the watch back and ran on.

Alice got up.

She has thought about this since, and she cannot say which part made her stand up. It was not the talking. It was the watch: a rabbit that is late is a rabbit that has somewhere to be, and Alice had nowhere to be at all that afternoon.

She ran after it across the field. The rabbit went under the hedge and she went under after it, and then it went down a large hole under a tree, and Alice went down too, without stopping to think how she would get out again.

The hole went straight down for a little way. Then it turned, and she was falling.

She fell slowly. That was the first odd thing and she noticed it at once: she had time to look about her. The sides of the well were full of cupboards and bookshelves, and here and there were maps and pictures hung on pegs. She took a jar off one of the shelves as she went past. It said ORANGE MARMALADE and it was empty, and she put it back on a lower shelf because she did not want to drop it on anybody below.

"Well!" she thought. "After a fall like this, I shall not mind falling down stairs at all."

She fell for a long time. She began to talk to herself, because there was nobody else.

"I wonder how many miles I have fallen. I must be near the centre of the earth. Four thousand miles down, I think — do you know, Dinah, I believe cats eat bats. Do cats eat bats?"

Dinah was her cat and was not there.

She was getting sleepy when the fall ended. It ended on a heap of sticks and dry leaves, and it did not hurt her.

She got up at once and looked about. There was a long low hall above her, lit by a row of lamps hanging from the roof, and at the far end of it the White Rabbit was going away in a hurry.

"Oh my ears and whiskers," it said, "how late it's getting!"

Alice ran. She was close behind it when it turned a corner, and she turned the corner too, and the Rabbit was gone.

She was alone in the long hall. There were doors all round it, and she went round and tried every one of them, and every one was locked.

In the middle of the hall there was a little three-legged table made of glass, and on the table there was nothing but a very small gold key.

### Bölüm 2: The Little Door

The key was too small for any of the doors, and Alice tried it in all of them twice.

Then, going round the hall for the third time, she found a low curtain she had not noticed, and behind the curtain there was a door about fifteen inches high.

The key fitted it.

She opened it and knelt down and looked along a passage no bigger than a rat-hole, and at the end of the passage there was a garden. She could see bright flowers and a fountain, and cool green grass.

She could not get her head through the door.

"And even if my head would go through," said Alice, "it would be of very little use without my shoulders."

She went back to the glass table, half hoping to find another key, and found instead a small bottle. There was a paper label round its neck, and the label said, in large letters, DRINK ME.

Alice was careful. She looked for the word "poison" first, because she had read several nice little stories about children who got burnt and eaten up by wild beasts because they would not remember the simple rules their friends had taught them. The bottle was not marked poison. So she drank it.

"What a curious feeling," she said. "I must be shutting up like a telescope."

She was. She was now ten inches high, and the right size to go through the little door.

She went to the door.

It was locked, and the key was on the glass table, and the glass table was now far above her head. She could see the key through the glass. She tried to climb one of the legs and it was too slippery. She sat down on the floor and cried a little, and then said to herself, quite sharply, "Come, there's no use in crying like that!" because she generally gave herself very good advice, though she very seldom followed it.

Then she saw a small glass box under the table, and in it a very small cake, with the words EAT ME marked on it in currants.

"I'll eat it," said Alice, "and if it makes me grow larger, I can reach the key; and if it makes me grow smaller, I can creep under the door. Either way I shall get into the garden."

She ate a little, and put her hand on the top of her head to feel which way it was going, and was surprised to find that she stayed the same size. This is what usually happens when one eats cake. So she finished it.

Then she grew. She grew until her head struck the roof of the hall, and she was more than nine feet high.

She took up the little gold key at once and went to the garden door, and lay down on the floor and looked at the garden through it with one eye, and it was further away than ever.

She sat down and began to cry again, and this time she cried a great deal, and there was a large pool round her, four inches deep, reaching half down the hall.

After a while she heard a small sound of feet, and she dried her eyes to see what was coming.

It was the White Rabbit, dressed, with white gloves in one hand and a large fan in the other, and it was in such a hurry that it dropped both of them and ran off into the dark.

### Bölüm 3: The Pool

She picked up the fan and the gloves, and because the hall was very hot she fanned herself while she thought.

She was talking to herself about whether she was still the same girl she had been that morning — she had tried to say her lessons and got them wrong, which was evidence one way — when she noticed that she had put on one of the Rabbit's little white gloves.

That was not possible at nine feet high. She had shrunk again to about two feet, and it was the fan doing it, and she dropped the fan very quickly, just in time.

Then her foot slipped, and she was in salt water up to her chin.

Her first thought was that she had fallen into the sea. Then she understood: it was the pool of tears she had cried when she was nine feet high, and she was now swimming about in it.

"I wish I hadn't cried so much," said Alice, swimming to look for a way out.

Something splashed near her. It was a mouse.

"O Mouse," said Alice, "do you know the way out of this pool? I am very tired of swimming, O Mouse!" She thought that was the right way to speak to a mouse; she had never done it before, but she had seen the list of cases in her brother's Latin grammar — a mouse, of a mouse, to a mouse, O mouse.

The Mouse looked at her and said nothing.

"Perhaps it doesn't understand English," thought Alice. "It may be a French mouse." So she said, out loud, the first sentence in her French lesson book: "*Où est ma chatte?*"

The Mouse gave a sudden leap out of the water and shook all over with fright.

"Oh, I beg your pardon," cried Alice. "I quite forgot you didn't like cats."

"Not like cats!" cried the Mouse. "Would *you* like cats if you were me?"

"Well, perhaps not," said Alice. "Don't be angry about it. And yet I wish I could show you our cat Dinah. She is such a dear quiet thing—" and Alice went on for some time about Dinah, sitting by the fire and washing her paws, and how good she was at catching mice, until the Mouse was swimming away from her as hard as it could go.

"I won't talk about her any more, if you'd rather not," Alice called.

"*We* won't talk about it," said the Mouse. "Come to the shore, and I'll tell you my history."

They swam to the shore. There was a crowd on it by then: a Duck and a Dodo, a Lory and an Eaglet, and several other curious creatures, all wet.

The first question was how to get dry. The Dodo said the best thing would be a Caucus-race.

"What *is* a Caucus-race?" said Alice.

"The best way to explain it," said the Dodo, "is to do it."

So it marked out a course, in a sort of circle — the exact shape did not matter, it said — and the party were placed along the course, here and there. There was no "One, two, three, and away." They began running when they liked and left off when they liked, so that it was not easy to know when the race was over. When they had been running half an hour and were quite dry, the Dodo called out, "The race is over!"

They all crowded round it, asking, "But who has won?"

The Dodo put one finger to its forehead and thought for a long time.

"*Everybody* has won," it said at last, "and all must have prizes."

Nobody in that crowd asked what the rule had been. They asked who had won, and then they asked what the prizes were, and Alice stood in the middle of them with wet shoes and did not ask either question out loud, because she had already worked out that asking it would not get her an answer.

### Bölüm 4: The House and the Glove

The White Rabbit came back along the path, looking for something, and talking to itself.

"The Duchess! The Duchess! Oh my dear paws! Oh my fur and whiskers! She'll have me executed, as sure as ferrets are ferrets! Where *can* I have dropped them?"

Alice understood that it was looking for the fan and the white gloves, and she began to look for them too, and they were nowhere: the hall had gone, and the glass table, and the little door, and she was standing under trees.

The Rabbit saw her.

"Why, Mary Ann!" it said angrily. "What *are* you doing out here? Run home this moment and fetch me a pair of gloves and a fan! Quick, now!"

Alice was so much frightened by the tone that she ran off at once in the direction it pointed, without trying to explain that it had made a mistake.

"It took me for its housemaid," she said to herself as she ran. "How surprised it will be when it finds out who I am. But I had better take it its fan and gloves — that is, if I can find them."

She came to a neat little house with a brass plate on the door. The name on the plate was W. RABBIT. She went in without knocking and up the stairs, into a tidy little room with a table by the window, and on the table there was a fan and two or three pairs of white kid gloves. She took the fan and one pair.

She was going out again when she saw a small bottle standing near the looking-glass. There was no label on it this time saying DRINK ME, but she uncorked it and put it to her lips anyway.

"Something interesting is sure to happen," she said, "whenever I eat or drink anything here. So I shall see what this bottle does."

It did something before she had drunk half of it. Her head pressed against the ceiling, and she had to stoop, and then to kneel, and then to lie down with one elbow against the door and the other arm out of the window and one foot up the chimney.

She had grown, and this time there was no more room to grow in.

"That was a great deal more than was wanted," said Alice.

She lay in that position for some time. It was very uncomfortable and there was no way out of it that she could think of; she was the size of the room.

Then things began to be thrown at the house from outside — she could hear voices arguing, and a ladder being put up, and somebody being sent onto the roof.

"Bill's got to go down the chimney," said a voice.

"I'm not going down the chimney," said another.

"Yes you are, Bill."

Alice, lying on the floor with her foot in the fireplace, listened to the whole discussion and understood that a lizard called Bill was being sent down a chimney that her own foot was blocking, and that nobody outside the house had asked whether there was anything in the chimney.

She waited until she heard the scratching just above her, and then gave one sharp kick upwards, and Bill went out of the chimney and over the heads of the crowd and into a cucumber frame, and there was a great deal of noise about it in the garden.

Nobody came in. Nobody knocked on the door of the room. They talked about her from the garden, in the way that men talk about a fallen tree that is lying across a road, and they sent for a wheelbarrow, and then they decided to burn the house down, and Alice called out of the window, "If you do that, I'll set Dinah at you!" — and the garden went completely silent for a moment, which was the only time in that whole afternoon that anything she said made any difference at all.

Then somebody said, "A barrowful of *what*?" and they started throwing pebbles.

One of the pebbles came in at the window and hit her, and she saw, when it landed on the floor beside her face, that it was not a pebble but a small cake.

### Bölüm 5: Advice from a Caterpillar

The cake made her small again, and she got out of the house and ran into the wood, and stopped when she was too tired to run.

She was about nine inches high. Everything in the wood was very large: the grass was over her head, and a daisy was a tree.

She came to a large mushroom, about the same height as herself, and on the top of it a caterpillar was sitting with its arms folded, smoking a long pipe and taking no notice of her at all.

They looked at each other for some time in silence.

"Who are *you?*" said the Caterpillar.

Alice answered, rather shyly, "I hardly know, sir, just at present. I know who I *was* when I got up this morning, but I think I have been changed several times since then."

"What do you mean by that? Explain yourself."

"I can't explain *myself*, I'm afraid, sir," said Alice, "because I'm not myself, you see."

"I don't see," said the Caterpillar.

"I'm afraid I can't put it more clearly," Alice said, very politely, "for I can't understand it myself; and being so many different sizes in a day is very confusing."

"It isn't," said the Caterpillar.

"Well, perhaps you haven't found it so yet," said Alice, "but when you have to turn into a butterfly, I should think you'll feel it a little queer, won't you?"

"Not a bit," said the Caterpillar.

That was the whole of that argument. The Caterpillar smoked for some minutes and then took the pipe out of its mouth and asked her what size she wanted to be.

"Oh, I'm not particular as to size," Alice said. "Only one doesn't like changing so often, you know. I should like to be a little larger, sir, if you wouldn't mind. Three inches is such a wretched height to be."

"It is a very good height indeed," said the Caterpillar, who was exactly three inches high, and reared itself upright as it spoke.

"But I'm not used to it," said Alice, and thought to herself that she wished the creatures were not all so easily offended.

The Caterpillar got down off the mushroom and crawled away into the grass. As it went, it said, without turning round:

"One side will make you grow taller, and the other side will make you grow shorter."

"One side of *what?*" thought Alice.

"Of the mushroom," said the Caterpillar, as if she had asked it out loud, and then it was gone.

Alice looked at the mushroom for a minute. It was perfectly round, and there was no way of telling which was one side and which was the other. She broke off a piece from each edge with both hands, so as to have both, and then had to decide which was which by trying.

She nibbled the right-hand piece.

Her chin struck her foot so hard that she had no time to be surprised. She got a little of the left-hand piece into her mouth with difficulty, and then her neck began to grow, and grew up out of the leaves like a stalk, until she was looking down on the tops of the trees with the wood far below her, and could not see her own shoulders at all.

A pigeon flew into her face and called her a serpent.

"I'm *not* a serpent," said Alice. "I'm a — I'm a—"

"Well! *What* are you?" said the Pigeon. "I can see you're trying to invent something."

"I'm a little girl," said Alice, doubtfully, remembering the number of changes she had gone through that day.

"A likely story indeed! I've seen a good many little girls in my time, but never *one* with such a neck as that. No, no! You're a serpent, and there's no use denying it. I suppose you'll be telling me next that you never tasted an egg."

"I have tasted eggs, certainly," said Alice, who was a truthful child, "but little girls eat eggs quite as much as serpents do."

"I don't believe it," said the Pigeon, "but if they do, then they're a kind of serpent, and that's all I can say."

Alice was so much surprised by this that she said nothing at all for a minute, and while she was silent the Pigeon added, "You're looking for eggs, I know *that* well enough; and what does it matter to me whether you're a little girl or a serpent?"

Alice went away and worked at the two pieces, a little of one and a little of the other, growing and shrinking, until she got herself down to about her usual height, which took a long time and left her holding two small pieces of mushroom, one in each hand.

She kept them both.

### Bölüm 6: Pig and Pepper

There was a house in the wood about four feet high, and two footmen were standing in front of it, one of them a fish and the other a frog.

The Fish-Footman took a large letter out from under his arm and gave it to the other, saying, in a solemn voice, "For the Duchess. An invitation from the Queen to play croquet."

The Frog-Footman repeated it in the same tone, changing the words round a little, and then they both bowed low and their curls got entangled.

Alice waited until the Fish had gone, and then went up to the door.

"There's no sort of use in knocking," said the Frog-Footman, "for two reasons. First, because I'm on the same side of the door as you are; secondly, because they're making such a noise inside that nobody could possibly hear you."

There was certainly a noise inside: a howling and sneezing, and every now and then a great crash, as if a dish had been broken.

"Please, then," said Alice, "how am I to get in?"

"*Are* you to get in at all?" said the Footman. "That's the first question, you know."

It was, no doubt: only Alice did not like to be told so. "It's really dreadful," she said to herself, "the way all the creatures argue. It's enough to drive one crazy."

The Footman sat down on the ground and looked up at the sky and said he should sit there, on and off, for days.

"But what am I to do?" said Alice.

"Anything you like," said the Footman, and began whistling.

So she opened the door herself and went in.

The kitchen was full of smoke. The Duchess was sitting on a three-legged stool in the middle, nursing a baby; the cook was leaning over the fire, stirring a large cauldron of soup; and there was a great deal of pepper in the air.

"There's certainly too much pepper in that soup," Alice said to herself, sneezing.

Even the Duchess sneezed occasionally, and the baby was howling and sneezing without any pause. The only two creatures in the kitchen that did not sneeze were the cook and a large cat, which was sitting on the hearth and grinning from ear to ear.

"Please would you tell me," said Alice, a little timidly, "why your cat grins like that?"

"It's a Cheshire cat," said the Duchess, "and that's why."

While they were speaking the cook took the cauldron of soup off the fire and began throwing everything within her reach at the Duchess and the baby: the fire-irons first, then a shower of saucepans, plates and dishes. The Duchess took no notice of them even when they hit her, and the baby was howling so much already that it was quite impossible to say whether the blows hurt it or not.

"Oh, *please* mind what you're doing!" cried Alice, jumping up and down.

"Here! You may nurse it if you like," the Duchess said, and threw the baby at her, and went out to get ready for croquet.

Alice caught it with some difficulty, as it was a queer-shaped little creature and held out its arms and legs in all directions. She carried it out into the open air, thinking that it would certainly be killed if she left it in that kitchen.

It grunted.

"Don't grunt," said Alice. "That's not at all a proper way of expressing yourself."

It grunted again, and she looked into its face to see what was the matter. It had a very turn-up nose, much more like a snout than a real nose, and its eyes were getting extremely small for a baby. She held it and looked at it for a minute or two.

Then it grunted again, so loudly that she looked down at it in some alarm, and this time there could be no mistake about it: it was a pig.

She set it down and it trotted away quietly into the wood.

"If it had grown up," Alice said to herself, "it would have made a dreadfully ugly child; but it makes rather a handsome pig, I think." And she began thinking of other children she knew who would do very well as pigs.

She was interrupted by the Cheshire Cat, which was sitting on the branch of a tree a few yards off.

"Would you tell me, please, which way I ought to go from here?" she asked.

"That depends a good deal on where you want to get to," said the Cat.

"I don't much care where—"

"Then it doesn't matter which way you go."

"—so long as I get *somewhere*," Alice added.

"Oh, you're sure to do that," said the Cat, "if you only walk long enough." It told her that a Hatter lived one way and a March Hare the other, and that she could visit either; they were both mad. Then it vanished, and appeared again a moment later, and asked whether she had said pig or fig; and then it vanished slowly, beginning with the end of the tail and ending with the grin, which stayed on the branch for some time after the rest of it had gone.

### Bölüm 7: A Mad Tea Party

There was a table set out under a tree in front of the house, and the March Hare and the Hatter were having tea at it. A Dormouse was sitting between them, fast asleep, and the two others were using it as a cushion, resting their elbows on it and talking over its head.

The table was large and laid for a great many people, and the three of them were crowded together at one corner of it.

"No room! No room!" they cried when they saw Alice coming.

"There's *plenty* of room," said Alice, and she sat down in a large arm-chair at the end.

"Have some wine," the March Hare said.

Alice looked all round the table, but there was nothing on it but tea. "I don't see any wine," she remarked.

"There isn't any," said the March Hare.

"Then it wasn't very civil of you to offer it," said Alice, angrily.

"It wasn't very civil of you to sit down without being invited," said the March Hare.

"I didn't know it was *your* table," said Alice. "It's laid for a great many more than three."

"Your hair wants cutting," said the Hatter. He had been looking at her for some time with great curiosity, and this was his first speech.

"You should learn not to make personal remarks," Alice said with some severity. "It's very rude."

The Hatter opened his eyes very wide, but all he said was, "Why is a raven like a writing-desk?"

"I believe I can guess that," said Alice.

"Do you mean that you think you can find out the answer to it?" said the March Hare.

"Exactly so," said Alice.

"Then you should say what you mean," the March Hare went on.

"I do," Alice replied, "at least — at least I mean what I say — that's the same thing, you know."

"Not the same thing a bit!" said the Hatter. "You might just as well say that 'I see what I eat' is the same thing as 'I eat what I see'!"

"You might just as well say," added the March Hare, "that 'I like what I get' is the same thing as 'I get what I like'!"

"You might just as well say," added the Dormouse, which seemed to be talking in its sleep, "that 'I breathe when I sleep' is the same thing as 'I sleep when I breathe'!"

"It *is* the same thing with you," said the Hatter, and there the conversation dropped, and the party sat silent for a minute.

The Hatter took his watch out of his pocket and looked at it uneasily, and shook it, and held it to his ear.

"What day of the month is it?" he asked Alice.

"The fourth," she said.

"Two days wrong!" sighed the Hatter. "I told you butter wouldn't suit the works." He looked at the March Hare angrily.

"It was the *best* butter," said the March Hare meekly.

Alice had been looking over his shoulder with some curiosity. "What a funny watch," she remarked. "It tells the day of the month, and doesn't tell what o'clock it is."

"Why should it?" muttered the Hatter. "Does *your* watch tell you what year it is?"

"Of course not," Alice replied, "but that's because it stays the same year for such a long time together."

"Which is just the case with *mine*," said the Hatter.

Alice was thoroughly confused, and said so.

The Hatter explained. He and Time had quarrelled, last March, at a concert given by the Queen of Hearts; and since the quarrel, Time would not do a thing he asked. It had been six o'clock in that garden ever since.

"Is *that* the reason so many tea-things are put out?" Alice asked.

"Yes, that's it," said the Hatter with a sigh. "It's always tea-time, and we've no time to wash the things between whiles."

"Then you keep moving round, I suppose?" said Alice.

"Exactly so," said the Hatter, "as the things get used up."

"But what happens when you come to the beginning again?" Alice ventured.

Nobody at that table answered her. The March Hare said he was tired of the subject and proposed that the Dormouse should tell them a story; and the Dormouse was woken by being pinched on both sides at once, and told a story about three little sisters who lived at the bottom of a well and were learning to draw, and when Alice asked what they drew, it said treacle, and when she asked how they could live at the bottom of a well, the Hatter told her to take some more tea.

"I've had nothing yet," said Alice, "so I can't take more."

"You mean you can't take *less*," said the Hatter. "It's very easy to take *more* than nothing."

Alice got up and walked away from the table, and neither of them called her back. When she looked round from the edge of the wood, they were trying to put the Dormouse into the teapot, and the table behind them was still laid for a great many people, and the clean cups at the far end had not been touched.

### Bölüm 8: The Queen's Croquet

There was a large rose-tree near the entrance of the garden, and three gardeners were painting the white roses on it red.

Alice went nearer to watch. She heard one of them say, "Look out now, Five! Don't go splashing paint over me like that!"

"I couldn't help it," said Five. "Seven jogged my elbow."

"That's right, Five," said Seven. "Always lay the blame on others."

"*You'd* better not talk," said Five. "I heard the Queen say only yesterday you deserved to be beheaded."

"What for?" said the one who had spoken first.

"That's none of *your* business, Two," said Seven.

"Yes, it *is* his business," said Five, "and I'll tell him — it was for bringing the cook tulip-roots instead of onions."

Alice asked, "Would you tell me, please, why you are painting those roses?"

Five and Seven said nothing and looked at Two. Two began, in a low voice, "Why, the fact is, you see, Miss, this here ought to have been a *red* rose-tree, and we put a white one in by mistake; and if the Queen was to find it out, we should all have our heads cut off, you know. So you see, Miss, we're doing our best, afore she comes, to—"

At that moment Five, who had been looking anxiously across the garden, called out "The Queen! The Queen!" and the three gardeners instantly threw themselves flat on their faces.

The procession came. First ten soldiers carrying clubs, then ten courtiers ornamented with diamonds, then the royal children, then guests, then the Knave of Hearts carrying the King's crown on a cushion, and last of all the King and Queen of Hearts.

The Queen stopped in front of Alice.

"Who is *this?*" she said, looking at the Knave, who only bowed and smiled.

"Idiot," said the Queen. "What's your name, child?"

"My name is Alice, so please your Majesty," said Alice very politely; and added to herself, "Why, they're only a pack of cards, after all. I needn't be afraid of them."

"And who are *these?*" said the Queen, pointing to the three gardeners lying round the rose-tree. As they were lying on their faces, and the pattern on their backs was the same as the rest of the pack, she could not tell whether they were gardeners, or soldiers, or courtiers, or three of her own children.

"How should *I* know?" said Alice. "It's no business of *mine*."

The Queen turned crimson with fury, and, after glaring at her for a moment like a wild beast, began screaming, "Off with her head! Off with—"

"Nonsense!" said Alice, very loudly and decidedly, and the Queen was silent.

The King laid his hand upon her arm and said timidly, "Consider, my dear: she is only a child."

The Queen turned away from him and told the Knave to turn the gardeners over. He did so, very carefully, with one foot. Then she gave the order for them to be beheaded and the procession moved on, and three of the soldiers stayed behind, and the gardeners ran to Alice for protection, and Alice put them into a large flower-pot that was standing near. The soldiers looked about for them for a minute or two and then marched quietly off after the others.

"Are their heads off?" shouted the Queen from the front of the procession.

"Their heads are gone, if it please your Majesty," the soldiers shouted back.

"That's right," shouted the Queen. "Can you play croquet?"

The game began at once, and it was the strangest croquet-ground Alice had ever seen in her life. It was all ridges and furrows. The balls were live hedgehogs, the mallets were live flamingoes, and the soldiers had to double themselves up and stand on their hands and feet to make the arches.

Alice's chief difficulty at first was in managing her flamingo. She got its body tucked under her arm with its legs hanging down, but just as she had got its neck straightened out and was going to give the hedgehog a blow with its head, it would twist itself round and look up in her face with such a puzzled expression that she could not help bursting out laughing; and when she had got its head down and was going to begin again, the hedgehog had unrolled itself and was crawling away. Besides all this, there was generally a ridge or a furrow in the way wherever she wanted to send the hedgehog, and, as the doubled-up soldiers were always getting up and walking off to other parts of the ground, Alice soon came to the conclusion that it was a very difficult game indeed.

The players all played at once, without waiting for turns, quarrelling all the while and fighting for the hedgehogs; and in a very short time the Queen was in a furious passion and went stamping about and shouting "Off with his head!" or "Off with her head!" about once in a minute.

Alice began to feel very uneasy. She had not had any dispute with the Queen yet, but it might happen any minute, and she looked about for some way of escape and wondered whether she could get out of the garden without being seen.

Then she noticed a curious appearance in the air, and after watching it a minute or two she made it out to be a grin, and said to herself, "It's the Cheshire Cat: now I shall have somebody to talk to."

"How are you getting on?" said the Cat, as soon as there was mouth enough for it to speak with.

Alice waited till the eyes appeared, and then nodded.

"It's no use speaking to it," she thought, "till its ears have come, or at least one of them." In another minute the whole head appeared, and then Alice put down her flamingo and began an account of the game, feeling very glad she had somebody to listen to her.

The Cat seemed to think that there was enough of it now in sight, and no more of it appeared.

"I don't think they play at all fairly," Alice began, "and they all quarrel so dreadfully one can't hear oneself speak — and they don't seem to have any rules in particular; at least, if there are, nobody attends to them."

### Bölüm 9: The Mock Turtle

The Duchess came out of the crowd and took Alice's arm and walked with her, and was extremely friendly, which was worse than the kitchen.

She had a moral for everything. Every time Alice said anything at all, the Duchess found a lesson in it and squeezed her arm.

"'Tis so," said the Duchess. "And the moral of that is — Oh, 'tis love, 'tis love, that makes the world go round."

"Somebody said," Alice whispered, "that it's done by everybody minding their own business."

"Ah, well! It means much the same thing," said the Duchess, digging her sharp little chin into Alice's shoulder, "and the moral of *that* is — Take care of the sense, and the sounds will take care of themselves."

Alice said nothing to this. She had begun to notice something about the morals: that any two of them could be exchanged without either being missed.

The Queen came past and the Duchess left in the middle of a sentence.

"A fine day, your Majesty!" said the Duchess, in a low weak voice.

"Now, I give you fair warning," shouted the Queen, stamping. "Either you or your head must be off, and that in about half no time. Take your choice."

The Duchess took her choice, and was gone.

"Come," said the Queen to Alice, "and I'll show you the Mock Turtle. It shall tell you its history."

She left Alice with a Gryphon and went away to see about some executions she had ordered. As soon as she was out of sight the Gryphon sat up and rubbed its eyes.

"What fun," it said.

"What *is* the fun?" said Alice.

"Why, *she*," said the Gryphon. "It's all her fancy, that: they never executes nobody, you know."

They came to the Mock Turtle, which was sitting sad and lonely on a ledge of rock, and it told Alice its history, which took some time, and the whole of it was about school.

"Once," it said, "I was a real Turtle. When we were little we went to school in the sea. The master was an old Turtle — we used to call him Tortoise."

"Why did you call him Tortoise, if he wasn't one?" Alice asked.

"We called him Tortoise because he taught us," said the Mock Turtle angrily. "Really you are very dull."

"We had the best of educations," it went on. "We went to school every day—"

"*I've* been to a day-school too," said Alice. "You needn't be so proud as all that."

"With extras?"

"Yes," said Alice. "We learned French and music."

"And washing?" said the Mock Turtle.

"Certainly not!" said Alice.

"Ah! Then yours wasn't really a good school," said the Mock Turtle, in a tone of great relief. "Now at *ours* they had, at the end of the bill, 'French, music, *and washing* — extra.'"

"You couldn't have wanted it much," said Alice, "living at the bottom of the sea."

"I couldn't afford to learn it," said the Mock Turtle with a sigh. "I only took the regular course."

"What was that?"

"Reeling and Writhing, of course, to begin with," the Mock Turtle replied, "and then the different branches of Arithmetic — Ambition, Distraction, Uglification, and Derision."

Alice did not dare to say she had never heard of Uglification, and asked instead what else they had learned.

"Mystery, ancient and modern," said the Mock Turtle, counting on its flappers, "with Seaography; then Drawling — the Drawling-master was an old conger-eel, that used to come once a week; *he* taught us Drawling, Stretching, and Fainting in Coils."

"How many hours a day did you do lessons?" said Alice.

"Ten hours the first day," said the Mock Turtle, "nine the next, and so on."

"What a curious plan!"

"That's the reason they're called lessons," the Gryphon remarked. "Because they lessen from day to day."

This was quite a new idea to Alice, and she thought it over a little before she made her next remark. "Then the eleventh day must have been a holiday?"

"Of course it was," said the Mock Turtle.

"And how did you manage on the twelfth?" Alice went on eagerly.

"That's enough about lessons," the Gryphon interrupted in a very decided tone. "Tell her about the games now."

So the Mock Turtle told her about the Lobster Quadrille, which is danced in a line along the sea-shore, and the two of them performed it for her, very solemnly, round and round, treading on her toes when they passed too close, and waving their forepaws to mark the time, and singing very slowly and sadly all the while.

When it was over the Mock Turtle sat down again and could not speak for some minutes, and Alice sat on the rock beside it and said, politely, that it had been very interesting, and did not say that neither of them had once explained what a Mock Turtle was, or asked her a single question about herself.

### Bölüm 10: Who Stole the Tarts?

The court was already sitting when they got there.

The King and Queen of Hearts were on their throne, with a great crowd assembled about them: all sorts of little birds and beasts, and the whole pack of cards. The Knave was standing before them, in chains, with a soldier on each side of him. Near the King was the White Rabbit, with a trumpet in one hand and a scroll of parchment in the other. In the very middle of the court was a table with a large dish of tarts on it, and they looked so good that they made Alice quite hungry to look at them.

"I wish they'd get the trial done," she thought, "and hand round the refreshments."

The twelve jurors were on a bench, writing on slates.

"What are they doing?" Alice whispered to the Gryphon. "They can't have anything to put down yet, before the trial's begun."

"They're putting down their names," the Gryphon whispered, "for fear they should forget them before the end of the trial."

"Herald, read the accusation," said the King.

The White Rabbit blew three blasts on the trumpet, unrolled the parchment, and read:

"The Queen of Hearts, she made some tarts, all on a summer day. The Knave of Hearts, he stole those tarts, and took them quite away."

"Consider your verdict," the King said to the jury.

"Not yet, not yet!" the Rabbit interrupted hastily. "There's a great deal to come before that."

"Call the first witness," said the King.

The first witness was the Hatter. He came in with a teacup in one hand and a piece of bread-and-butter in the other, and said he had not finished his tea.

"You ought to have finished," said the King. "When did you begin?"

The Hatter looked at the March Hare, who had followed him into court. "Fourteenth of March, I *think* it was," he said.

"Fifteenth," said the March Hare.

"Sixteenth," said the Dormouse.

"Write that down," the King said to the jury; and the jury eagerly wrote down all three dates on their slates, and then added them up, and reduced the answer to shillings and pence.

"Give your evidence," said the King, "or I'll have you executed, whether you're nervous or not."

"I'm a poor man, your Majesty," the Hatter began, in a trembling voice, "and I hadn't but just begun my tea — not above a week or so — and what with the bread-and-butter getting so thin — and the twinkling of the tea—"

"The twinkling of *what?*" said the King.

"It *began* with the tea," the Hatter replied.

"Of course twinkling begins with a T!" said the King sharply. "Do you take me for a dunce? Go on."

"I'm a poor man," the Hatter went on, "and most things twinkled after that — only the March Hare said—"

"I didn't," the March Hare interrupted in a great hurry.

"You did," said the Hatter.

"I deny it," said the March Hare.

"He denies it," said the King. "Leave out that part."

The Hatter was allowed to go, and went, and was still hurrying out of the court when the Queen said, "And just take his head off outside," which nobody did.

The next witness was the Duchess's cook, who brought the pepper-box with her, and made the whole court sneeze, and said that tarts were made mostly of pepper, and that she would not give any other evidence, and left.

"Call the next witness," said the King. He looked at the White Rabbit, and added in a low voice: "Really, my dear, *you* must cross-examine the next witness. It quite makes my forehead ache."

The White Rabbit looked at the list, and Alice watched him, wondering who it could be.

Then the White Rabbit read out, at the top of his shrill little voice, the name "Alice!"

### Bölüm 11: The Verdict

"Here!" cried Alice, and forgot in the excitement how large she had grown in the last few minutes, and jumped up in such a hurry that she tipped over the jury-box with the edge of her skirt, upsetting all the jurymen onto the heads of the crowd below.

"Oh, I *beg* your pardon!" she cried, and began picking them up again as quickly as she could.

"The trial cannot proceed," said the King, "until all the jurymen are back in their proper places — *all*," he repeated with great emphasis, looking hard at Alice.

Alice looked at the jury-box and saw that she had put the Lizard in head downwards, and the poor little thing was waving its tail about in a melancholy way. She got it out and put it right, and thought that it did not much matter which way up it was.

"What do you know about this business?" the King said to Alice.

"Nothing," said Alice.

"Nothing *whatever?*" persisted the King.

"Nothing whatever."

"That's very important," the King said, turning to the jury. They had just begun to write this down on their slates when the White Rabbit interrupted: "*Un*important, your Majesty means, of course."

"Unimportant, of course, I meant," the King said hastily, and went on to himself in an undertone, "important — unimportant — unimportant — important—" as if he were trying which word sounded best.

Some of the jury wrote it down "important," and some "unimportant." Alice could see this, as she was near enough to look over their slates.

"Silence!" said the King, and read out from his book: "Rule Forty-two. *All persons more than a mile high to leave the court.*"

Everybody looked at Alice.

"I'm not a mile high," said Alice.

"You are," said the King.

"Nearly two miles high," added the Queen.

"Well, I shan't go, at any rate," said Alice. "Besides, that's not a regular rule: you invented it just now."

"It's the oldest rule in the book," said the King.

"Then it ought to be Number One," said Alice.

The King turned pale and shut his note-book hastily. "Consider your verdict," he said to the jury, in a low trembling voice.

"There's more evidence to come yet, please your Majesty," said the White Rabbit, jumping up in a great hurry. "This paper has just been picked up."

"What's in it?" said the Queen.

"I haven't opened it yet," said the White Rabbit, "but it seems to be a letter, written by the prisoner to — to somebody."

"It must have been that," said the King, "unless it was written to nobody, which isn't usual, you know."

"Who is it directed to?" said one of the jurymen.

"It isn't directed at all," said the White Rabbit. "In fact, there's nothing written on the *outside*." He unfolded the paper as he spoke, and added, "It isn't a letter after all: it's a set of verses."

"Are they in the prisoner's handwriting?" asked another of the jurymen.

"No, they're not," said the White Rabbit, "and that's the queerest thing about it."

The jury all looked puzzled.

"He must have imitated somebody else's hand," said the King. The jury all brightened up again.

"Please your Majesty," said the Knave, "I didn't write it, and they can't prove I did: there's no name signed at the end."

"If you didn't sign it," said the King, "that only makes the matter worse. You *must* have meant some mischief, or else you'd have signed your name like an honest man."

There was a general clapping of hands at this: it was the first really clever thing the King had said that day.

"That *proves* his guilt, of course," said the Queen. "So, off with—"

"It doesn't prove anything of the sort!" said Alice. "Why, you don't even know what they're about!"

The verses were read. They were about nothing at all that anybody in that court could connect with a dish of tarts, and when they were finished the King said that it was the most important piece of evidence they had heard yet, and told the jury to consider their verdict.

"No, no!" said the Queen. "Sentence first — verdict afterwards."

"Stuff and nonsense!" said Alice loudly. "The idea of having the sentence first!"

"Hold your tongue!" said the Queen, turning purple.

"I won't!" said Alice.

"Off with her head!" the Queen shouted at the top of her voice.

Nobody moved.

"Who cares for *you?*" said Alice, who had grown to her full size by this time. "You're nothing but a pack of cards!"

At this the whole pack rose up into the air and came flying down upon her; and she gave a little scream, half of fright and half of anger, and tried to beat them off, and found herself lying on the bank with her head in her sister's lap, while her sister brushed away some dead leaves that had fluttered down from the trees onto her face.

"Wake up, Alice dear," said her sister. "What a long sleep you've had!"

"Oh, I've had such a curious dream," said Alice, and she told her sister as much of it as she could remember.

Her sister listened, and kissed her, and said, "It *was* a curious dream, dear, certainly; but now run in to your tea: it's getting late."

So Alice got up and ran off, thinking while she ran, as well she might, what a wonderful dream it had been.

Her sister sat still with her head on her hand, watching the sun set, and thinking of little Alice and all her wonderful adventures, until she half believed herself in Wonderland — and knew that she had only to open her eyes again and everything would change back to dull reality: the grass would be only rustling in the wind, and the pool rippling to the waving of the reeds; the rattling teacups would change to tinkling sheep-bells, and the Queen's shrill cries to the voice of the shepherd boy — and the sneeze of the baby, the shriek of the Gryphon, and all the other queer noises would change, she knew, into the confused clamour of the busy farmyard, while the lowing of the cattle in the distance would take the place of the Mock Turtle's heavy sobs.

The hut turned on its chicken legs. Baba Yaga looked down at the girl. "You want fire," she said. "First you will work. Clean my house. Wash my clothes. Sort this bag of seeds — all before morning. If the work is not done, I will eat you."

The work was too much for ten people. Vasilisa fed the doll a piece of bread. "Sleep," said the doll. "Morning is wiser than evening." While the girl slept, the doll worked. In the morning everything was done.

Baba Yaga looked at the work for a long time. "You have questions," she said. "Ask."

They do not explain the rule. They only say that Vasilisa's grandmother knew it, and Vasilisa lived. And the children ask: is it true? And the grandmothers put another log on the fire and say: feed your doll, and you will never need to know.

## The Country That Eats Breakfast Twice

Kimlik 1063 · A2 · 322 kelime · 2 dk

Visitors in Madrid often notice the same strange thing: at half past seven in the morning, the streets are dark and almost empty. Cafés open late, offices fill slowly, and breakfast seems to be missing. It is not missing. In Spain, breakfast simply happens twice — and the first one is very small.

### Two Breakfasts

The first breakfast, at home, is often just coffee with milk and maybe a biscuit or a piece of toast. The real one comes later, at about half past ten, when the working day stops for a pause called the almuerzo de media mañana. Offices empty, and the bars fill with people eating pan con tomate — bread with olive oil and fresh tomato — or a small sandwich. This is why lunch in Spain can wait until two or three in the afternoon, later than almost anywhere in Europe, and why dinner often starts at nine or ten at night. The whole Spanish day runs late — but it runs on a system.

### The Wrong Clock

Part of the answer hangs on the wall. Geographically, Spain sits in line with Britain and Portugal, so its natural time zone is London's. But in 1940, the government moved the country's clocks one hour forward, to match Berlin — and after the Second World War, the clocks never moved back. Since then, the Spanish sun has been "late" by the clock: in Madrid in summer, it can stay light until almost ten at night. People did not change their nature; they follow the sun, not the number. By the sun, a Spanish lunch at two o'clock is really a European lunch at one.

### The Morning Test

The system has one simple rule that surprises visitors most: a big early breakfast is seen as unnecessary, because the mid-morning pause is never far away. The Spanish morning is not slow. It is patient — it knows the second breakfast is coming.

## The See-Through Umbrella

Kimlik 1067 · A2 · 313 kelime · 2 dk

When it rains in Tokyo, the street changes colour. Millions of umbrellas open at the same time — and almost all of them are the same one: round, plastic, and completely see-through. In many countries, umbrellas are black or colourful. In Japan, the city under the rain looks like a river of glass.

### An Invention from 1958

The see-through umbrella is a Japanese invention. A small umbrella company in Tokyo, White Rose, made the first one in 1958, using a new material: soft, clear vinyl. At first, shops did not want it. Then, in 1964, the Olympic Games came to Tokyo, foreign visitors saw the strange clear umbrella, and it became famous. It also solved a real problem. Tokyo is one of the most crowded cities in the world, and on a narrow street full of people, a black umbrella covers the eyes. Through a clear one, walkers can see the person coming — so millions of people can pass each other in the rain.

### The 500-yen Umbrella

The second reason is the price. In every convenience store in Japan, a plastic umbrella waits by the door for about 500 yen — the price of a cup of coffee. When the rain starts, people simply buy one. When the rain stops, many forget it in the train or leave it at the office. Japan sells roughly 120 to 130 million umbrellas every year, more than one for almost every person in the country — and most of them are the clear plastic kind.

### The Umbrella Mountain

The forgotten ones all go to the same place. At the lost-and-found office of Tokyo's police, umbrellas are the number one lost item: hundreds of thousands arrive every year, a small mountain of clear plastic. Only a few owners ever come back for them. A 500-yen umbrella, it seems, is easy to love and easy to forget.

## The Slowest Express in the World

Kimlik 1088 · A2 · 325 kelime · 2 dk · günlük havuz

There is a famous train in Switzerland called the Glacier Express. The name sounds fast. The train is not fast. It travels about 290 kilometres in eight hours — about 36 kilometres per hour. A bicycle rider can go faster. People call it the slowest express train in the world.

### Slow on Purpose

The train is slow because the Alps are in the way. Between the towns of Zermatt and St. Moritz, the line climbs and falls all day. The train crosses 291 bridges and goes through 91 tunnels. At its highest point, the Oberalp Pass, it is 2,033 metres above the sea. In some steep places, a special toothed rail under the train holds it like a zip, so it cannot slide back down the mountain.

But there is a second reason. The train is slow because slow is the product. Nobody takes the Glacier Express to save time; there are faster ways across Switzerland. People pay for the eight hours, not in spite of them. The windows are huge and curve up into the roof. Passengers sit under glass and watch the mountains change for a whole day: snow peaks, green valleys, and rivers the colour of milk.

### The Glass of Wine

The train is so smooth and so slow that lunch is served at the seats, with real plates and real glasses. In the shop, tourists buy a famous souvenir: a wine glass with a bent stem. It leans to one side, like the train on a mountain curve. It is a joke about the ride — but on the steepest parts of the line, a normal glass really does look like it is standing at an angle.

The Glacier Express opened in 1930. Back then, the trip took eleven hours. After almost a hundred years of better engines and better rails, the company has made the journey three hours shorter — and not one minute more. Fast was never the plan.

"I shall put nothing in it," said Anne, "except what is written down. I shall be a machine. It will be the plainest cake ever made in the history of Avonlea, and it will be perfect, and Mrs. Allan will ask me for the receipt, and I shall say, quite simply, *it is nothing, it is only a layer cake*, and I have practised saying that."

The cake rose beautifully. It came out of the oven high and even and golden, and Anne put the icing on it and set it in the pantry under a cloth.

At four o'clock Mr. and Mrs. Allan came. Mrs. Allan was young and had a nice laugh. Anne handed things, and did not talk too much, and got through the bread and butter, the cold tongue, the preserves, the cookies and the doughnuts, and then the cake was brought in and cut, and Mrs. Allan took a large piece and put a fork into it and took one mouthful.

Her face did something very small and very quick.

"Anne Shirley," said Marilla, who had taken a mouthful too, "what did you put in that cake?"

"Vanilla," said Anne. "Only vanilla. Two teaspoons, out of the brown bottle on the second shelf."

Marilla went to the pantry and came back with the brown bottle from the second shelf and held it out. There was a yellowed label on it and the label said *Best Anodyne Liniment*.

Marilla had broken her old vanilla bottle three weeks before and had poured the liniment into an empty vanilla bottle, and had meant to write a new label, and had not.

Anne sat down on the nearest chair and put her face in her hands.

"Liniment," she said. "I have made a cake with liniment in it and given it to a minister's wife."

"You are not to cry about it," said Marilla.

"I am not crying. I am past crying. This is the most tragic thing that has ever happened to anybody in this room and I shall remember it when I am eighty."

Mrs. Allan put her fork down and laughed — not politely; properly, with her head back.

"My dear child, you must not think anything more about it. I put linseed oil in a pudding once at my own mother's table and served it to nine people."

"You did not."

"I did. And two of them ate the whole plate to be kind, which was worse." She looked at the cake. "Come and show me your garden instead. I am told there is a place with a name."

Anne got up. She was still white, but she got up, and took Mrs. Allan out to the front, and by the gate she had begun again.

"That is the White Way of Delight," she said, "and the little pond down there is the Lake of Shining Waters, and those birches are the Snow Queens, and I have not named the hill yet because it has not told me what it is."

"The Snow Queens," said Mrs. Allan. "That is exactly what they are."

Anne stopped walking. Then she went on again.

After the Allans had gone, Marilla was washing the dishes and Anne was drying, and neither of them said anything for a while.

"Marilla. Am I to be punished?"

"Punished for what? I poured liniment into a vanilla bottle and did not label it," said Marilla. "You put in two teaspoons of exactly what you were told to put in. — Dry the big plate; it goes on the top shelf."

Anne dried the big plate.

"Tomorrow I shall make another cake," she said, "and I shall smell everything in the pantry first, one bottle at a time, out loud."

"That will be a sensible arrangement," said Marilla.

### Bölüm 7: The Ridgepole

Josie Pye had a way of daring people that was not friendly.

It happened in August, in the Barrys' garden, with six girls there. Josie walked the top of the board fence, all the way round, without falling off, and then said that walking a fence was nothing and that she supposed nobody there would dare walk the ridgepole of the Barrys' kitchen roof.

"That is not brave," said Anne. "That is only high."

"Then you will not do it," said Josie.

"I did not say that."

"You will not do it."

Diana said, "Anne. Don't."

Anne was already going up the ladder that the men had left against the kitchen roof.

She got onto the ridgepole. She stood up on it, and took four steps along it, and on the fifth step the roof was steeper than she had understood from the ground, and there was nothing anywhere to put a hand on, and she went down the slope of the roof and off the edge of it and into the Virginia creeper below.

The six girls made a great deal of noise. Diana got there first and Anne's eyes were shut.

Mr. Barry carried her into the house. Her ankle was broken.

Marilla came across the field without a coat, which the neighbours talked about for a week, because Marilla Cuthbert had never in her life gone out of her own door without a coat.

The doctor came, and set it, and said seven weeks.

Anne lay on the sofa in the Green Gables sitting room for seven weeks. It was the longest seven weeks in the history of the world, she said, and she said it about once a day.

What she had not expected was the traffic.

Diana came every afternoon. Mrs. Allan came nine times, and brought a book each time, and did not talk about the roof. Mrs. Lynde came and said that a girl who walked on a ridgepole was asking for a broken ankle, and then stayed an hour and a half and told Anne fourteen pieces of village news. Josie Pye came once, with a paper bag of apples, and did not mention the dare, and Anne did not mention it either, and they got through the visit.

Gilbert Blythe did not come, but a slate arrived on the second week with no name on it, brand new, wrapped in brown paper, and Anne looked at it for a while and then had Marilla put it on the top shelf of the cupboard, unused.

Matthew came in and out. He did not sit down. He looked out of the window and said things about the crop, and left an apple on the arm of the sofa most days, and one evening he brought in a bunch of very late wild roses that he had walked half a mile for, and put them in a jam jar of water beside her without saying anything about it.

On the last evening before she was allowed up, Marilla came in with the lamp.

"Anne. Would you do it again?"

Anne thought about it seriously, which was one of her habits, and answered honestly, which was another.

"I would not walk that ridgepole again," she said. "But I would not be able to say no in front of six people. That is the part I cannot fix. The roof I can fix."

Marilla put the lamp on the table.

"There is a difference between being brave and being dared," she said. "You are eleven; you may take some years over learning it. — Mrs. Allan left a book on the chair."

"Will you be cross if I say something about the seven weeks?"

"That depends what it is."

"Everybody came," said Anne. "Fourteen people came. I have never been in a house where people came about me."

Marilla went out to the kitchen and shut the door, and stayed there for some time, and came back with nothing in her hands.

### Bölüm 8: Green Hair

Anne's hair was the one thing she never made a name for.

In April a pedlar came to the door with a case of things: needles, buttons, cheap lace, and small bottles. Marilla was out. Anne bought a bottle for seventy-five cents — which was the whole of the money she had saved from Christmas — and the pedlar said it would turn her hair a beautiful raven black, and that it was used by ladies in Charlottetown, and that there was no smell to it worth mentioning.

She put it on in the afternoon, in her own room, with a small brush, following the directions.

Her hair turned green.

Not black, and not red: a dull dark green, the colour of pond weed, all over, evenly, because Anne had been thorough.

She washed it seven times.

At supper she did not come down. Marilla went up and found her sitting on the bed in the dark with a towel over her head.

"Anne. Come down to supper."

"I am never coming down again. You may bring my meals up for the rest of my life. I cannot be looked at."

Marilla took the towel off.

She stood there for a moment, and then she said, "Well." And then, after another moment: "Green."

"You may say it. Say that I am a wicked, vain girl and that this is what comes of it."

"I was not going to say that," said Marilla. "I was going to say that it will have to be washed, and that seven times is not enough, and that we shall try soap and hot water tomorrow, and then we shall see."

They washed it every day for a week: soap, then soft soap, then a thing Mrs. Lynde recommended and would not name. It stayed green.

On the Saturday Marilla sat down on the chair by the bed with the scissors in her lap.

"It will have to be cut off," she said. "All of it. It will grow."

"How long?"

"A year before it is anything."

Anne sat very still. Then she said, "Do it before I have time to make a speech about it," and Marilla did it, and it took twenty minutes, and neither of them talked at all while it was happening.

When it was done Anne looked in the glass once and then turned the glass to the wall.

"You may keep the mirror turned round for a week," said Marilla, "and then you will turn it back yourself."

On Monday Anne went to school in a hat that she did not take off. Josie Pye said something at recess and Diana said something back, and Anne said nothing, which was new.

The worst part, she told Diana in June, was not the school. The worst part was that she had done it to herself, and that there was nobody to be angry with, and that being angry with yourself has nowhere to go.

Her hair came in that autumn a good deal darker than it had been, and by the following spring it was a deep red-brown that two people in the village told her was handsome, and one of them was Mrs. Lynde, who had never handed out a compliment without a hook in it before.

Anne wrote in a notebook that year a list of things she was giving up, and there were four items on it, and the fourth was *dyeing my hair*, and the first three were crossed out later and the fourth never was.

### Bölüm 9: The Story Club

The Story Club had four members: Anne, Diana, Ruby Gillis and Jane Andrews. It met on Saturday afternoons in the Barrys' orchard when it was fine and in the Green Gables kitchen when it was not, and each member had to write one story a week and read it aloud.

The rules were Anne's. Every story had to have a name for the place it happened in. Nobody was allowed to say a story was bad after it had been read, but anybody could say which part they believed.

Ruby's stories were all about people dying beautifully. Jane's were sensible and had no adjectives in them and were about girls who did their work and were rewarded. Diana's had good beginnings and no endings; she got to the middle and stopped, and said the middle was the interesting part anyway. Anne's stories had four characters, a lake, and somebody who was very sorry at the end, and were three times as long as anybody else's.

In November Anne read one out that had two sisters in it who were separated for eleven years, and Ruby cried at the end, and Jane said the part she believed was the bit where the older sister could not remember which hand the younger one wrote with.

"That is the part I made up last," said Anne.

"That is often the way," said Jane.

They sent one of Anne's stories in to a magazine in Charlottetown in December, all four of them signing a letter to go with it, and it came back in February in the same envelope with a printed slip in it, and the slip did not have a name on it either.

The club met the next Saturday in the kitchen.

"We shall not send any more," said Ruby. "It is not worth being made to feel like that."

"I shall send another one in April," said Anne.

"Why?"

"Because the slip is printed," said Anne. "They print those slips by the hundred and put them in the envelopes without reading anybody's name. That means it was not personal. If it had been personal they would have written it out by hand."

Jane said, "That is either very clever or the most foolish thing I have ever heard."

"It is both," said Marilla, from the stove, where she was making bread and had not been supposed to be listening.

They kept the club up for two years. Anne sent three more stories away and got three more printed slips, and kept all four of them in the back of the notebook, and did not tell Diana that she kept them.

What came out of the Story Club, in the end, was not a story. It was Miss Stacy.

Miss Stacy came as the new teacher in the autumn and was twenty-four and had been to Queen's, and she found out about the club in her second week, and asked to hear one, and sat on a desk with her boots swinging and listened to a whole story of Anne's without saying anything until the end.

Then she said: "There is too much in it. You have put everything you have into one story because you are afraid there will not be another. There will be another. Take three things out and read it to me again next Saturday."

Anne took three things out. It was better. She has said since that this was the first useful sentence anybody ever said to her about writing, and that she did not enjoy it.

### Bölüm 10: Matthew Buys a Dress

Marilla made Anne's dresses herself, out of good wincey, and they were plain and they fitted and they had no puffed sleeves.

Every other girl in the Avonlea school had puffed sleeves that year.

Anne did not ask. She had asked once, in September, and Marilla had said that sleeves like that took a yard and a half of material to hold up nothing at all, and Anne had accepted the arithmetic and had not brought it up again. But in November she stood in front of the glass in a new brown dress with tight sleeves and said to Diana afterwards, in the lane, that she could get through the whole of life without puffed sleeves and would rather not have to.

Matthew heard that. He was mending harness on the other side of the hedge and he was not supposed to be there and he heard the whole of it.

For three weeks he thought about it. Then, on a Saturday in December, he put on his good coat and drove to Carmody, to William Blair's store, to buy a dress with puffed sleeves for a girl of thirteen.

There was a woman behind the counter. Matthew had not counted on a woman behind the counter.

"Good afternoon, Mr. Cuthbert. What can I get you?"

"Any — any garden rake," said Matthew.

She got him a garden rake, in December, and he paid for it.

He drove home, put the rake in the barn where Marilla would not see it for a month, and sat in the kitchen that evening turning the problem over.

On the Monday he went to Mrs. Lynde's, which took a considerable amount of doing, and stood in her kitchen with his hat in his hand and said:

"Mrs. Lynde, I want to get a dress for Anne for Christmas and I cannot buy it myself. There is a woman in the store."

Mrs. Lynde, who had been waiting nine months for Matthew Cuthbert to need something, was extremely pleased.

"What kind of dress?"

"The kind with the sleeves," said Matthew. "The sleeves that go out."

"Puffed sleeves."

"Yes."

"Brown gloria, and I shall make it up myself, and I shall not tell Marilla," said Mrs. Lynde. "And Matthew Cuthbert, if you had come to me about the child's clothes two years ago it would have saved everybody a great deal of trouble."

The dress was finished on the twenty-third and hidden in Mrs. Lynde's spare room, and Matthew carried it home in the dark on Christmas Eve and put it under the tree in the sitting room, which Anne had made out of a small fir and a great deal of tissue paper.

On Christmas morning she came down and found it: brown gloria, with a frill, and sleeves that stood out at the shoulder like two puffs of smoke.

She did not say anything for about four seconds, which for Anne was a long time.

"Oh, Matthew," she said. "Oh, Matthew."

"Well now," said Matthew, "I thought — well now."

Marilla came in from the kitchen, looked at the sleeves, and said, "There is a yard and a half of material in each of those and no use in either of them."

"Yes," said Anne.

"Well," said Marilla. "You had better put it on before church and not spill anything on it. — Matthew, there is a rake in the barn that has been there since December and I should like to know what it is for."

Anne wore the dress to the Christmas concert that night and recited a piece in it, and Matthew sat in the fourth row and did not clap at the end because he had forgotten to, and Mrs. Lynde, beside him, said, "Well?" and Matthew said, "Well now, I guess our Anne did about as well as any of them."

### Bölüm 11: The Scholarship

Miss Stacy started the Queen's class in the autumn: extra lessons after school, four days a week, for the pupils who meant to try the entrance examination for Queen's Academy and become teachers.

There were ten in it. Anne was in it. Gilbert Blythe was in it.

They studied for a year, and in that year Anne and Gilbert did not speak once, and both of them came first in something every month.

The examinations were in Charlottetown in July. Anne stayed with Aunt Josephine Barry for the week and wrote papers in a hot hall with two hundred other people, and came out of the last one not able to say a single thing about how it had gone.

The results were printed in the Charlottetown paper three weeks later.

Diana came up the lane at a run with the paper and got as far as the gate and could not speak, and Anne took it out of her hands.

There were two hundred names in the list. The first name was Gilbert Blythe. Beside it, on the same line, was Anne Shirley.

They had tied for first place.

Anne sat down on the step with the paper on her knees.

"Both," said Diana. "Both of you. Anne, they never do that. There has never been a tie."

Matthew came round the corner of the house and was told, and said "Well now" four times, and went to the barn and stayed there for twenty minutes.

Marilla read the list herself, with the paper held out at arm's length, and then folded it and put it behind the clock, which was where Marilla kept things that were not to be thrown away.

"I knew it," she said. "I have known it since the year she came. — There is a great deal of work to do before September."

At Queen's, Anne took the two-year course in one year, which was allowed and was not often done, and at the end of it she won the Avery scholarship: two hundred and fifty dollars a year for four years, at Redmond College, to study for a B.A.

She came home in June with the news and a medal in a small box.

They sat in the kitchen that night, the three of them, with the lamp between them.

"Four years," said Marilla.

"Four years," said Anne, "and I shall come home every summer, and I shall write on Sundays, and Matthew, you are not to do the far field by yourself while I am gone; you are to hire Jerry Buote for the whole of the haying and not for half of it."

"Well now," said Matthew.

"He will hire him," said Marilla.

Matthew went out at about nine to see to the horses. He stopped at the door and turned round with his hand on the latch.

"Anne. I would not want a dozen boys instead of you. It was not a boy that took the Avery scholarship, was it? It was a girl — my girl — that I am proud of."

And he went out before anybody could answer, which was his method.

Anne sat looking at the door for a while.

"He has been practising that," said Marilla. "He has been going about the place for three days with his mouth moving."

"How do you know?"

"Because I have lived with him for fifty-five years," said Marilla, "and because I have been practising one too, and mine is not ready."

### Bölüm 12: The Bend in the Road

Matthew died on a Monday afternoon in the last week of June.

He had come up from the field with a folded newspaper in his hand — there had been news in it about a bank in Charlottetown, the bank the Cuthberts' money was in, and it had failed — and he got as far as the doorstep and put his hand on the door frame and went down.

The doctor came and said it had been his heart and that it had been quick.

There were three days of people in the house. Anne handed things and did not cry in front of anybody, and on the second night, upstairs, she said to Marilla that there was something wrong with her because she could not cry.

"It will come," said Marilla.

It came the next morning, at the sight of a white rose in the garden, which was the kind he had put in the jam jar the summer she broke her ankle.

After the funeral the two of them sat in the kitchen and did the arithmetic, because it had to be done.

The bank had gone. What was left was Green Gables and the stock and Marilla's eyes, which had been getting worse for a year and about which the specialist in town had said: no sewing, no reading by lamplight, no strain, or she would lose them.

Marilla said she would sell the farm and go and board somewhere.

"You will not," said Anne.

"I cannot run it alone and I cannot see to do the fine work. You are going to Redmond in September."

"I am not going to Redmond," said Anne. "I have thought about it for four days and I have written two letters this morning. I have given up the Avery. I am going to teach — there is the school at Carmody and I can get it, or there is Avonlea if Gilbert gives it up, which he will not. I shall teach and board at home and read for my B.A. by correspondence, and it will take longer, and I shall have it."

"Anne Shirley. I will not let you give up your scholarship for me."

"You cannot stop me," said Anne. "I am sixteen and I have posted the letters."

Marilla put her hand over her eyes, which she did that year for two reasons and it was not always possible to tell which.

"When you came here," she said, "I meant to do my duty by you. That was the whole of it: duty. I have never told you that I love you, because I was not brought up to say such things and I did not know how to begin. But you are as dear to me as my own flesh and blood and you have been ever since you came to Green Gables."

Anne knelt down by the chair and put her face against Marilla's hands.

A week later Gilbert Blythe gave up the Avonlea school so that Anne could have it and take it walking, and went to White Sands instead, which was six miles from his own house.

Anne found out from Mrs. Lynde, and she went out to the gate at sunset and stood there, and when Gilbert came down the road she said his name.

He stopped.

"Gilbert. I want to thank you for giving up the school for me."

"It was nothing."

"It was six miles each way," said Anne. "That is not nothing; that is arithmetic. — I have been horrid to you for five years and you sent me a slate when I broke my ankle and I never said one word about it."

"You kept it, though."

"I kept it on the top shelf," said Anne. "Unused. Which is worse."

Gilbert laughed, and they stood at the gate and talked for half an hour about Queen's and the schools and the examination they had tied in, and Anne got in at half past eight, and Marilla said, "Who was that at the gate?"

"Gilbert Blythe."

"You stood there half an hour."

"We had five years to get through," said Anne.

That night she sat at the window of the east gable with the lamp out. The road went down the hill and around a bend where the trees hid it.

She had meant to go to Redmond and be a B.A. in four years, and instead she was going to teach fifteen children in Avonlea and read Latin at night and mind a woman's eyes.

She had used to think that the road ran straight and that she could see the whole of it. She could not see past the bend now, and she found, sitting there, that she did not want to.

## The Wonderful Wizard of Oz

### Bölüm 1: The Grey House

Dorothy lived on the flat grey prairie in Kansas with her Uncle Henry and her Aunt Em.

The house had one room. It had four walls, a floor, a roof, a stove, a cupboard, three beds, and a table, and there was nothing else in it, because there was no money for anything else and nowhere to put it.

Everything outside was grey. The grass was grey. The ploughed earth was grey. The house had been painted once and the sun had taken the paint off it, so the house was grey too.

Uncle Henry did not laugh. He worked from morning until dark and he looked at the sky a great deal. Aunt Em had been pretty when she came to Kansas and the sun and the wind had changed that, and when Dorothy laughed she was surprised, because she could not see what there was to laugh at.

Toto made Dorothy laugh. Toto was a small black dog with long hair and very small black eyes, and he was not grey, and Dorothy loved him.

One afternoon Uncle Henry stood up and looked at the sky and said, "There is a cyclone coming, Em."

The long grass went down flat in a wave. The sky in the north went the wrong colour. Uncle Henry ran for the animals and Aunt Em opened the trap door in the floor and went down the ladder into the small dark hole under the house, and called for Dorothy.

Toto jumped out of Dorothy's arms and went under the bed.

She went after him. She got him by the ear and carried him back to the trap door, and the house shook twice, and then the floor came up.

The cyclone had picked the house up.

It went up with the wind and stayed up. The house turned round two or three times and then went along steadily, high in the air, in the middle of the storm, and the wind held it there.

Dorothy sat on the floor and waited. Then she got up and looked out. Then she put Toto in the bed and got in beside him.

The house rocked. Toto got out of bed once and fell through the open trap door, and Dorothy thought he was gone; and then his ears came up out of the hole, because the air was pressing up under him and holding him there, and she reached in and pulled him back and shut the door.

Hours went by. Dorothy went to sleep, in a flying house, in a cyclone, which shows what kind of girl she was.

She woke up because the house had come down with a bump.

The sun was coming in at the window. Dorothy got up with Toto behind her and opened the door.

She stood in the doorway and did not say anything for a while.

There was green grass, and trees with fruit on them, and a stream with blue water, and birds in the trees with red and yellow feathers, and the air was full of the sound of them.

Behind her, through the door, was the one grey room.

Toto ran out and rolled in the grass, and Dorothy came down the step in her blue and white dress, and while she stood there a group of people came up the road toward her: three men and one woman, no taller than Dorothy, dressed in blue, with round hats with bells round the brims.

They stopped a little way off and took their hats off and bowed.

"You are welcome," said the oldest of them, "and we thank you very much for killing the witch."

### Bölüm 2: The Silver Shoes

"I have not killed anybody," said Dorothy.

"Your house has," said the old man, and pointed.

Under the corner of the house, sticking out, were two feet in silver shoes.

"Oh dear," said Dorothy. "The house must have fallen on her. What shall we do?"

"There is nothing to be done," said the old man, and he seemed quite happy about it.

The people in blue were called the small folk of the east, and the woman who had been under the house had been their witch and had kept them working for her for many years, and now they were free, and they said so, and they said it in the tone of people giving out good news at a market.

Then another person came down the road: a woman in a white dress with a pointed hat, who came walking and did not seem to touch the ground much.

"I am the witch of the north," she said. "I am a good one. You have killed the witch of the east and you have set these people free. Where do you come from?"

"From Kansas," said Dorothy.

"I have never heard of Kansas. Is it a civilised country?"

"Yes," said Dorothy.

"Then that explains it," said the witch of the north. "In the civilised countries there are no witches left. We have four here. Two are good and two are bad, and one of the bad ones is under your house."

While they were talking, the feet in the silver shoes went away. There was nothing left where they had been except the shoes themselves, standing side by side on the grass.

"The shoes are yours," said the witch of the north, and picked them up and handed them over. "They belonged to her. There is some charm about them, but nobody now knows what it is."

Dorothy took the silver shoes into the house and put them on the table. Then she washed her face, and ate some bread from the cupboard, and gave Toto some, and came out again.

"I want to go back to Kansas. Aunt Em will not know what has become of me."

The people in blue shook their heads.

"There is a desert on the east, and a desert on the west, and a desert on the north, and a desert on the south," said the witch of the north, "and nobody crosses them. You will have to stay with us."

Dorothy sat down on the step and cried, and the people in blue took out their handkerchiefs and cried too, out of politeness.

Then the witch of the north took off her hat, balanced the point of it on her nose, and said, "One, two, three," and the hat became a slate with writing on it.

The writing said: LET DOROTHY GO TO THE CITY OF EMERALDS.

"Is that Kansas?"

"No. It is where the great Oz lives. He is a great wizard and he is the only one who could send you back. It is a long way and the country is sometimes pleasant and sometimes dark. I cannot go with you and I cannot protect you, but I will give you this."

She kissed Dorothy on the forehead. Where her lips touched there was a round shining mark.

"Nobody will dare to hurt a person who has been kissed by the witch of the north. Follow the road of yellow brick. When you get to Oz, do not be afraid of him; tell him your story and ask him for help. Goodbye, my dear."

The people in blue bowed to her and went away among the trees, and the witch of the north turned round three times on her left heel and was gone, which surprised Toto very much, and he barked at the place where she had been.

Dorothy put on the silver shoes, because her own shoes were old and the silver ones fitted exactly, which is the kind of thing that happens in that country.

Then she took her bread in a basket, and shut the door of the house, and went to look for the road.

### Bölüm 3: The Man of Straw

The road of yellow brick began at the edge of the blue country and went west.

Dorothy walked all morning. She passed neat blue houses with round roofs, and the people who lived in them came out and looked at the mark on her forehead and bowed and did not stop her.

She slept that night in a house where a family gave her supper, and in the morning she went on.

By noon she came to a fence, and behind the fence was a field of corn, and in the field was a pole with a man on it.

He was made of a sack stuffed with straw, with a painted face, a blue pointed hat, blue clothes and old boots, and he was up on a pole to keep the birds off, and the birds were sitting on him.

Dorothy stopped and looked at him. He looked back, and winked.

"Good day," said the Scarecrow.

"Did you speak?" said Dorothy.

"Certainly. How do you do?"

"I am pretty well, thank you," said Dorothy politely. "How do you do?"

"I am not feeling well," said the Scarecrow, "because it is very dull to be up on a pole all day and night with nothing to do."

"Can you get down?"

"No. I am on a nail."

So Dorothy lifted him off the nail, and he came down light as a bundle of clothes and stood up and stretched himself, and said, "Thank you. Who are you and where are you going?"

"I am Dorothy, and I am going to the City of Emeralds to ask the great Oz to send me back to Kansas."

"Where is Kansas?"

"That is where I live," said Dorothy, "and it is grey."

The Scarecrow thought about that. "Why should you want to go back to a grey place?" he said.

"Because it is home."

"That is a reason I do not follow," said the Scarecrow, "and I have no brains, so I shall not argue about it. — Do you think this Oz would give me some brains?"

"I could not say. He might."

"Then I shall come. Being stuffed with straw, I cannot get hurt, and if my legs come off you can put them back on."

They went on together. The Scarecrow had trouble with the bricks and fell into holes, and Dorothy pulled him out again, and he was cheerful about it every time.

They talked, and while they were talking he told her, without being asked and without complaining, the plain history of his life: he had been made two days before; he had been put on the pole; a crow had sat on his shoulder that afternoon and had told him that if he had brains he would be a man like other men, and this was the only sentence anybody had ever said to him.

"I know I have no brains," he said. "I do not mind about my head being stuffed with straw. What I mind is that a man of straw is not asked for his opinion."

"I am asking you," said Dorothy. "The road turns two ways up there. Which one?"

The Scarecrow looked at the two ways for some time.

"The right one," he said. "The other one goes down into the trees and it is getting late."

They took the right one, and it was the correct choice, and he did not say anything more about it.

At sunset they came to a small empty cottage by the road, and Dorothy went in and slept on a bed of dry leaves.

The Scarecrow could not sleep. He stood in the corner of the room all night, being careful not to move, because Toto was asleep near his foot.

### Bölüm 4: The Man of Tin

In the morning they went on, and the country got wilder. The trees came close to the road and the fruit on them was not the kind you eat.

At about ten o'clock Dorothy heard a sound in the wood: a low groan, not far off.

They went in among the trees and found a man standing beside a half-cut log with an axe in his hands, up in the air, over his head.

He was made of tin. His arms and legs were jointed, his head was a tin can with a face, and he had a funnel for a hat.

He did not move. He was standing exactly as he had been standing for a long time.

"Did you groan?" said Dorothy.

"Yes," said the Tin Man. "I have been groaning for a year and nobody has come."

"What can I do for you?"

"Get an oil can and oil my joints. They are rusted so badly that I cannot move at all. There is an oil can in my cottage."

Dorothy went to the cottage and got the oil can, and came back, and asked him where to begin.

"My neck first."

She oiled his neck, and the Scarecrow took hold of the tin head and turned it gently from side to side until it went easily.

"Now the arms."

She oiled the shoulders and the elbows, and the Scarecrow worked them up and down, and the Tin Man lowered the axe and leaned it against the tree and let his arms hang, which he had not done for a year.

"Now the legs."

When it was finished he walked round in a circle twice and sat down on the log.

"That is a great comfort," he said. "I have been holding that axe up since the rain last spring. — It was careless of me to be caught in the rain. It is the sort of thing I do."

He asked where they were going and Dorothy told him.

"Do you think Oz would give me a heart?" said the Tin Man.

"He might. It would be as easy as giving the Scarecrow brains."

"That is true," said the Tin Man. "Then I will come, if you will let me. — You had better take the oil can. If I get caught in the rain again I shall want it, and I cannot reach my own shoulders."

Dorothy put the oil can in her basket.

He told them about himself as they walked, and he told it in the plain way, the way a person tells the history of a house.

He had not always been tin. He had been a woodman with a body, and he had wanted to marry a girl, and there had been an accident with his own axe — and then another, and another — and a tinsmith had made him a new arm, and then a new leg, and then the other leg, and then a head, and had done all of it very well.

"And then my chest," he said. "He made me a new chest and it was a good one. But he forgot to put a heart in it, and I did not notice for some weeks, and by then he had gone away and I did not know where."

"Did you go back to the girl?"

"She is still there," said the Tin Man. "She lives with her mother at the other end of the wood. I did not go, because a man with no heart cannot love anybody, and it would not have been fair to her."

He said that, and then he stopped talking about it, and about half an hour later he stopped walking, and went down on his knees in the road and picked up a beetle that had got itself onto the yellow bricks, and put it off to one side in the grass where nothing would step on it.

Then he got up and they went on, and nobody said anything about the beetle at all.

### Bölüm 5: The Lion

The road went into a dark wood in the afternoon, and the trees met over the top of it, and the three of them walked closer together than they had been walking before.

Something roared, and a very large lion came out of the trees into the road in one jump.

He knocked the Scarecrow over with one paw, spinning him along the bricks, and he struck the Tin Man, who fell down flat and made a great noise, and the Lion drew back from the noise and looked at his own paw.

Then Toto ran at him, barking, and the Lion opened his mouth to eat the dog.

Dorothy ran forward and slapped the Lion on the nose as hard as she could.

"Do not you dare to bite Toto! You ought to be ashamed of yourself, a big beast like you, biting a little dog!"

"I did not bite him," said the Lion, rubbing his nose with his paw.

"You tried to. You are nothing but a big coward."

"I know it," said the Lion, and his head went down. "I have always known it. How did you find out?"

"Have you no dignity at all?"

"None," said the Lion. "I roar and everything runs away, and I have never had to do anything else, and if anything ever stood still I do not know what I should do. — Everybody says the lion is the king of beasts. I have thought about it a great deal and it seems to me that I am not."

The Scarecrow got up and pushed his own straw back into his shoulder. The Tin Man was helped up and made to walk about until the dents stopped catching.

"You did not hurt them," said Dorothy. "One is straw and one is tin."

"That is fortunate," said the Lion, and he meant it, and he said it in the tone of a person who has just got out of some trouble.

So he came with them, and it was arranged in about four sentences, and none of them was a speech.

He walked on the outside, next to the trees, which he did without being asked and did every day after that.

That evening, in a clearing, the Scarecrow made the fire because the Tin Man was afraid of rust and would not go near it, and the Lion lay down with his back to the dark and said:

"If Oz can give a brain and a heart, do you think he could give me courage?"

"He could give you courage as easily as brains," said the Scarecrow.

"Or a heart," said the Tin Man.

"Or send me back to Kansas," said Dorothy.

The Lion put his chin down on his paws.

"Then that is four of us," he said, "and one road," and he went to sleep.

In the night Toto woke up and went out of the firelight after something, and there was a sound from the trees.

The Lion was up before Dorothy had her eyes open, and out at the edge of the clearing with his back to the camp, and he stood there looking into the dark, and he was shaking from his shoulders to his tail, and he did not move from the spot until the sound had gone away.

Then he came back and lay down again and said, "It was nothing. A bird."

Nobody in the clearing said anything about the shaking, then or afterwards.

### Bölüm 6: The River

On the fourth day the road came to a river, and the road went into the water on one side and came out of it on the other, and there was no bridge.

The river was wide and it was moving.

"We must build a raft," said the Scarecrow. "The Tin Man has an axe."

So the Tin Man cut small trees and the Scarecrow fetched them, which took him twice as long and he did not stop, and by evening there was a raft, and they slept on the bank and went on to the water in the morning.

They pushed off with poles. In the middle of the river the current took the raft and turned it, and began to carry it down, away from the yellow road on the other side.

The Scarecrow put his pole down into the water and held on, and the raft went out from under him, and he was left standing in the middle of the river holding a pole, with the water going past him and his feet off the bottom.

"Goodbye," he called after them, because he could see what was going to happen and there was nothing else to say.

The raft went down the river with the other three on it and came in against the bank a long way below the road.

The Lion swam out and pulled the raft in with a rope in his teeth, which took a good while, and Dorothy and the Tin Man got onto the grass.

Then they walked back up the bank until they could see the Scarecrow, out in the water, on his pole, a long way from either side.

He looked very lonely.

"What can we do?" said the Tin Man.

A stork came flying up the river, and stopped in the air, and looked at them.

"Who are you and where are you going?"

"I am Dorothy and these are my friends and we are going to the City of Emeralds," said Dorothy, "and our friend is out there on a pole."

"Why does he not walk?"

"He is stuffed with straw," said Dorothy. "If he gets wet he will be finished."

The stork looked out at the river.

"He is heavy for me," she said, "and I will try."

She flew out, took the Scarecrow by the arm, brought him in over the water and dropped him on the bank, and the Scarecrow got up and thanked her, and she said it was nothing and that she had a nest to get back to, and went off up the river.

Then the four of them walked back along the bank toward the road, and it took the rest of the afternoon, and the road was exactly where they had left it.

"We have lost a day," said Dorothy.

"We have," said the Scarecrow. "And I have thought of something while I was standing on that pole. There was nothing to do out there and it was the first time I have had nothing to do since you took me off the nail, and it turns out that when there is nothing to do I think about the same thing over and over."

"What thing?"

"Nothing worth telling," said the Scarecrow. "Let us get on. The road goes up over that hill and I should like to see what is on the other side of it before dark."

They got on.

### Bölüm 7: The Field of Flowers

On the other side of the hill the country was green and flat, and there were red flowers in it, and further on there were more red flowers than grass.

The road went straight through the flowers and the smell of them came up warm out of the ground.

"These are poppies," said the Lion. "If a person sleeps among them he does not wake up."

"Then we must walk fast," said the Scarecrow.

They walked fast. The flowers got taller and the road got harder to see under them, and Dorothy began to want to sit down, and then she did not want anything at all except to close her eyes, and she stopped walking and lay down in the flowers with Toto beside her, and both of them were asleep.

"We are close to the end of the field," said the Scarecrow. "I can see grass ahead."

"Carry her," said the Tin Man.

The Scarecrow and the Tin Man made a chair of their hands and carried Dorothy, and Toto in her lap, and walked on, and the Lion walked beside them and said nothing, and then the Lion said, "I am going to run," and he ran.

He ran a long way and they saw him get smaller among the flowers, and then they did not see him.

They came out of the poppies onto the grass by the river and put Dorothy down where the air was clean, and she opened her eyes after a while and sat up.

"Where is the Lion?"

The Scarecrow pointed back at the field.

They could see him a long way in, lying on his side in the red flowers, and he was too big to carry.

They sat on the grass and looked at him.

A field mouse came running across the grass with a wildcat behind her, and the Tin Man put out his axe and the wildcat stopped and thought about it and went away.

The mouse smoothed her fur.

"I am the Queen of the Field Mice," she said, "and you have saved my life, and I should like to do something for you."

"Our friend is asleep in the poppies and he is a lion," said Dorothy.

The mouse looked at the field for some time.

"How many mice would that take?"

"A great many," said the Scarecrow.

"Then there will be a great many," said the Queen, and she squeaked three times, and mice came out of the grass until the grass was moving all over.

The Tin Man cut wood and made a truck with four wheels, and the Scarecrow tied every mouse to the truck with a piece of string, and the mice pulled it into the field, and the Scarecrow and the Tin Man rolled the Lion onto it, and the mice pulled him out.

It took until the sun was low.

When the Lion was on the clean grass the Queen untied her string herself and said that a debt was a debt and now it was paid, and she gave Dorothy a small whistle and told her to blow it if she was ever in trouble, and then the mice went back into the grass and the grass stopped moving.

The Lion woke up in the evening and stood and stretched.

"I ran as far as I could," he said.

"You did," said the Scarecrow.

The Lion looked at the truck with the string on it, and at the mice tracks in the mud, and at Dorothy holding a whistle, and he did not ask anything, and Dorothy did not tell him.

They slept there, and in the morning the road went on toward a green light in the sky.

### Bölüm 8: The Green City

The green light in the sky was a wall, and the wall was green, and the gate in it was green and had emeralds on it, and a man in green stood in front of the gate.

"We wish to see Oz," said Dorothy.

"Nobody has asked me that for years," said the man. "He is terrible. Why do you want him?"

"I want to go home."

The man looked at her, and looked at the Lion, and went into a little room and came back with a box of spectacles with green glass in them, and locked a pair onto every one of them, including Toto, because he said the light of the city would blind them otherwise.

Then he opened the gate and the city was green.

The houses were green marble and the streets were green stone and the people wore green clothes and sold green sweets and green lemonade, and there was no other colour anywhere, and Dorothy looked through her green glass at all of it and did not say that it might only be the glass.

They were put in four rooms with green beds and green sheets, and a girl in green brought Dorothy a green dress and washed it afterwards, and the Tin Man was oiled, and the Scarecrow ate nothing and stood by the window all night looking at the street, and in the morning the soldier came and said Oz would see one of them each day.

Dorothy went first.

The throne room was round and green and empty, and in the middle of it there was a chair, and on the chair there was a head with no body, and the head opened its eyes and looked at her.

"I am Oz," said the head. "Why do you seek me?"

She told him.

"Kill the Witch of the West," said the head, "and I will send you home. Until then, no."

The Scarecrow went the next day and found a lady in the chair with green silk and jewels, and she said the same thing about the Witch, and the Tin Man went the day after and found a beast with five legs and a head like a rhinoceros, and it said the same thing about the Witch, and the Lion went last and found a ball of fire burning in the middle of the room with nothing holding it up, and a voice out of the fire said the same thing about the Witch.

They met afterwards in the green room.

"He was a head," said Dorothy.

"She was a lady," said the Scarecrow.

"It was a beast," said the Tin Man.

"It was fire," said the Lion, "and it made me sit down."

They put the four things side by side in their minds and then set them down again, because none of it changed the sentence at the end.

"He wants the Witch killed," said the Tin Man.

"I have never killed anything," said Dorothy, "and I do not know how a person begins."

"You begin by going west," said the Scarecrow. "After that there will be something in front of you and you will do that."

The Lion said that he was frightened, and that he was going, and that both of those were true at once and he had stopped expecting one of them to fix the other.

In the morning the soldier took them to the western gate, unlocked the spectacles off their faces and put them back in the box, and the city behind them was green anyway, or looked green, and Dorothy did not turn round to check.

The country outside the west gate was brown.

### Bölüm 9: The West

They walked west for two days and there was no road, only brown grass, and then a hill of yellow rock, and the Witch saw them from her castle window before they saw anything of hers.

She had one eye and it was long-sighted.

She sent wolves, forty of them, and the Tin Man stood in the gap in the rocks with his axe and the wolves came at him one at a time because the gap was narrow, and when the sun came up there were forty wolves on the ground and the Tin Man's arm was tired.

She sent crows, and the Scarecrow stood up and spread his arms and caught them as they came, and the crows had nothing to fear from a man of straw and were wrong about that.

She sent bees, and the Scarecrow pulled the straw out of himself and covered the others with it, and the bees stung the Tin Man and broke their stings on him and died in a heap, and the others put the straw back into the Scarecrow, and it took a while and he said that his left leg was in his chest and they fixed it.

Then she sent her own soldiers, and the Lion stood in front and roared, and the soldiers ran away.

So the Witch put on her Golden Cap and called the Winged Monkeys, and the monkeys came in a dark line across the sky.

They took the Tin Man up high and dropped him on the rocks and he did not get up. They pulled the straw out of the Scarecrow and threw his clothes into a tree. They tied the Lion with ropes and carried him to the castle yard.

Then they picked Dorothy up carefully and did not hurt her at all, because there was a mark on her forehead where the good witch had kissed her and no monkey would touch that, and they set her down in the yard and went away.

The Witch looked at the mark on her forehead and did not touch her either.

She put Dorothy to work in the kitchen, and she put the Lion in the yard with no food to make him pull a wagon, and Dorothy carried him food at night, and the Lion said that he would not pull anything, and the Witch said that then he would be hungry, and both of them kept their word for many days.

The Witch wanted the silver shoes. She could not take them while Dorothy was awake and she could not go into the room where Dorothy slept because of the mark.

So she stood by while Dorothy scrubbed the floor and told her about the wolves and the crows and the bees, and how many days the Lion had been hungry, and how long a person can live in a kitchen.

One afternoon she reached down and took hold of the shoe on Dorothy's left foot.

Dorothy had a bucket of water in her hand.

She threw it, and the water went over the Witch from her hat to her shoes.

The Witch said, "See what you have done," and she began to get smaller, and then to get flat, and then there was a brown thing on the floor like a bag, and a wet place around it, and the shoe was standing by itself with nothing holding it.

Dorothy got another bucket and washed the wet place out of the door, because it was on the floor she had just cleaned.

Then she went out to the yard and untied the Lion.

"She is finished," said Dorothy.

"How?"

"Water."

The Lion thought about this and did not say anything about it, and they went to look for the others.

### Bölüm 10: Behind the Curtain

They found the Tin Man on the rocks, bent in three places, and they carried him to the castle yard where the tinsmiths worked, and the tinsmiths hammered him straight over two days and he stood up and was a little thinner in one arm and said it did not matter.

The Scarecrow's clothes were in the top of a tall tree. The Lion pushed the tree over and the Tin Man cut it up, and they filled the clothes with fresh straw from the Witch's stable, and the Scarecrow said this straw was better than his old straw and that he would rather not think about where his old straw had gone.

In the Witch's cupboard Dorothy found the Golden Cap, and she put it in her basket without knowing what it did.

The people of the castle were glad, and gave them bread and cheese and a walking stick for the Lion, who did not want one, and they went east for three days, and the yellow bricks came back under their feet on the fourth, and the green wall of the city came up out of the flat country on the fifth.

The soldier at the gate locked the spectacles on again.

Then they waited. Oz did not send for them. They waited two days, and three, and on the fourth day the Scarecrow said that a man who keeps you waiting is deciding something, and Dorothy sent the soldier in with a message saying that if Oz did not see them she would ask the Winged Monkeys to bring him, and the soldier came back very quickly and said they were to go in at once.

The throne room was empty. There was no head and no lady and no beast and no fire, only the green room and the chair.

"I am here," said a voice from up near the ceiling. "Ask what you wish."

"We wish what you promised," said Dorothy. "The Witch is finished."

"Come back tomorrow. I must have time to think."

"You have had four days," said the Tin Man, and the Lion roared, one short roar, because the room was small.

Toto jumped at the noise and knocked over a screen that stood in the corner, and the screen went down flat.

Behind it there was a little old man with a bald head and a wrinkled face, and he was holding a speaking trumpet, and he stood there and looked at the four of them looking at him.

"I am Oz," he said. "Please do not shout."

Nobody shouted.

"Are you not a great wizard?"

"Not in the least. I am a common man from Omaha and I was a balloonist and the balloon came down here, and the people thought I was a wizard because I came out of the sky, and they built me this city and I let them wear green glasses and I have been in this room ever since, and I have been very much afraid of the Witch of the West for a long time."

"Then the head?"

"Paper and paint."

"The fire?"

"A ball of cotton and oil on a wire."

"You are a bad man," said the Tin Man.

"I am a very good man," said the little old man, "and a very bad wizard, and both of those have always been true."

"And my brains?" said the Scarecrow.

The little man sat down on the edge of the chair and put the speaking trumpet on the floor beside him.

"Come tomorrow morning," he said, "all four of you, and I will do what I can do, and I would rather you did not tell the city."

They said they would not, and went out, and in the corridor the Scarecrow stopped and looked back at the closed door for a moment and then walked on and did not say what he was thinking.

### Bölüm 11: The Gifts

In the morning the little man was waiting with a pair of scissors and a paper bag.

He took the Scarecrow's head off, and took the straw out of it, and mixed bran with pins and needles in the bag, and put the mixture in, and sewed the head shut.

"You have bran-new brains," he said.

The Scarecrow put his hands up to his head and felt it, and walked to the window, and said that he could feel the pins, and that he had a thought coming, and stood there having it.

Then the Tin Man came, and the little man cut a square hole in his tin chest with the scissors and set inside it a small heart made of red silk and filled with sawdust, and soldered the square back on.

"Is it a kind heart?"

"It is a very kind heart," said the little man. "You will have to take my word for that part."

The Tin Man put his hand flat on the place where the solder was still warm and left it there.

The Lion came last, and the little man poured something out of a green bottle into a green dish and told him to drink it.

"What is it?"

"Courage. It is only courage while it is inside you, so drink it quickly."

The Lion drank it and licked the dish and said he felt full of courage, and the little man said that was the correct feeling.

Then there was Dorothy.

"Kansas is a long way," said the little man, "and I cannot make a hole in the sky. But I came here in a balloon and I can make another one, and there is silk in this city and there is glue, and if the wind is right the balloon will go the way it came."

They made the balloon for three days. Dorothy glued and the Scarecrow cut the silk in straight lines because his pins made him careful, and the Tin Man built a basket out of a clothes basket and rope, and on the fourth day the balloon stood in the square in front of the palace full of hot air, tied down with a rope, pulling.

The city came to watch. The little man told the people that he was going to visit a brother wizard in the clouds and would return, and the people said that they would wait, and some of them cried.

"Come along, Dorothy," said the little man from the basket. "Quickly."

Dorothy went to get Toto, who had run after a kitten in the crowd, and she caught him and turned round with him in her arms, and while she was turning the rope broke.

The balloon went up. It went up fast, and the little man leaned over the edge and said something that nobody heard, and the balloon got smaller and went east into the clouds and did not come back.

The crowd stood in the square looking up for a while, and then went home.

Dorothy sat down on the palace steps with Toto in her lap.

The Scarecrow sat down next to her and did not say that the balloon would have burst, or that the wind had been wrong, or anything else about the balloon.

After a while the Tin Man said, "There is a witch in the south. Glinda."

"How far?"

"Nobody knows," said the Lion. "South."

Dorothy stood up and shook out her dress.

"Then we will need bread," she said, and went in to get some.

### Bölüm 12: Three Steps

They went south. The country got warmer and the fields had fences and the fences had gates, and on the second day they came to a wood where the trees hit them with their branches and would not let them through, and the Scarecrow walked up to the biggest tree and was thrown back, and got up, and said that there was a place further along where the trees were thinner because he had watched which branches moved, and there was, and they went through it.

Then there was a wall of white china, and beyond it a country of china houses and china people the height of Dorothy's knee, and they walked through it very carefully and knocked over a china cow's leg all the same, and the milkmaid was angry, and they paid for the leg with a piece of gold from the Witch's castle and got out at the other side.

Then there were hills with hammer-headed men in them who butted anyone who climbed, and they were butted twice, and then Dorothy remembered the Golden Cap in her basket, and the writing inside it, and she read the words out and the Winged Monkeys came.

"You have called us twice more than this," said their leader, "and this is the third and last. Where?"

"South. To Glinda."

The monkeys carried the four of them over the hills all afternoon and set them down at the edge of a country of red flowers and red fences, in front of a red road, and went away, and that was the end of the cap.

Glinda's palace was red inside and out and she sat in a red chair with red hair and she was young and she looked at the four of them for a while before she spoke.

Dorothy told her all of it, from the house to the balloon.

"I can send you home," said Glinda. "The silver shoes will carry you anywhere in three steps. You have been wearing them since the first morning."

Dorothy looked at her shoes.

"Then I could have gone home from the cornfield," she said.

"You could," said Glinda, and said nothing else about it, and asked the Scarecrow what he wanted.

He wanted the Emerald City, because the people had asked him to rule it while Oz was away and he thought he could do it now. The Tin Man wanted the country of the West, because the people there had asked him and he liked being asked. The Lion wanted the wood in the south where the animals had said they needed a king, and he said he was still frightened of the wood and was going to it.

Glinda said the monkeys would carry each of them where he was going, using the cap, which she now owned, and she would give the cap back to the monkeys afterwards so that nobody would ever use it again.

Then there was the goodbye.

Dorothy put her arms round the Lion's neck and rubbed his ears. She kissed the Tin Man, and he cried and she wiped his face with her apron so that he would not rust, and she held the Scarecrow's soft body and did not want to let it go, and none of them said anything about brains or hearts or courage.

"Knock the heels together three times," said Glinda, "and say where you want to go."

Dorothy took Toto up in her arms, and looked at the three of them once, and knocked the heels of the silver shoes together three times.

"Take me home to Aunt Em."

She was in the air and then she was sitting on the Kansas grass in front of a new farmhouse, and the shoes had come off somewhere on the way and were not anywhere, and Aunt Em came out of the door with the milk pail in her hand and stopped.

"Where in the world did you come from?"

"From the country of Oz," said Dorothy, "and here is Toto, and Aunt Em, I am so glad to be at home."

## The Secret Garden

### Bölüm 1: Nobody Left

Mary Lennox was the most disagreeable-looking child anybody had ever seen, and there was a reason for it, and nobody in that house had ever thought about the reason.

She was born in India. Her mother was very pretty and went to parties. Her father was busy and ill. Mary was given to an Indian nurse who was told that if she wanted to please the mistress she must keep the child quiet and out of sight, so the nurse gave Mary whatever she wanted, and Mary got everything she asked for and never once got what she needed.

By the time she was nine she was thin and yellow, with thin fair hair and a cross mouth, and she had never dressed herself, and she had never played with another child.

One very hot morning she woke up and there was a different servant standing by her bed.

"Where is my Ayah? Why has she come? I will not have her. Send my Ayah."

The woman said something about the Ayah not being able to come.

Mary was told nothing else that day, and nobody came to her room after breakfast, and she went out into the garden by herself and played under a tree, making a flower bed in the earth by sticking hibiscus blossoms into little heaps of soil.

She could hear people running in the house. She could hear somebody crying — not the polite crying of a servant who has been scolded: the other kind.

She stayed under the tree.

There was cholera in the house. It had come in the night, and it went through that household in a day, and Mary sat in the garden and made her flower bed and was not told.

By the afternoon it was quiet.

She fell asleep under the tree and woke in the evening and went in through a side door, and there was nobody in the hall, and nobody in the dining room, and food on the table that had been left where it was.

She sat down and ate a peach and some biscuits and drank a glass of wine, because it was there and she was thirsty, and then she went to sleep in a chair.

She woke because two men had come into the room, and one of them was a young officer she had never seen.

"How sad. There was a child. Somebody said there was a child."

"There was a child," said the other. "Nobody ever saw her. There is nothing here now."

And Mary stood up in the middle of the room in her nightgown and said, "I am Mary Lennox. I fell asleep when everybody had the cholera, and I have only just woken up. Why does nobody come?"

The young officer went the colour of the wall, and turned to the other man, and said one sentence very quietly, and the sentence was: "The poor little kid. There is nobody left to come."

So that is how Mary Lennox found out, standing on a rug in an empty room, and she did not cry, then or later, and I am going to put down what she thought about instead, because she told it years afterwards and it is exact.

She thought: nobody has come for two days.

She thought: nobody came for two days before that either, and I did not notice.

She was taken to an English clergyman's house for a week, where there were five children who played in a garden and called her Mistress Mary Quite Contrary to her face, and she disliked all of them, and then she was put on a ship to England, to an uncle she had never met, because he was the only person left who was obliged to have her.

### Bölüm 2: A Hundred Rooms

The train went north for a long time and it was raining when she got out.

Mrs. Medlock, the housekeeper, met her at the station: a large woman with a hard face and a black bonnet, who had come all the way to London to fetch her and was not pleased about it.

"I may as well tell you something before we start," she said in the carriage. "You are not going to a cheerful place. — Mr. Craven will not see you. He does not want to. His back is crooked and he has been a sour young man and got no good of his money until he married. His wife died, and now he goes away most of the year and when he is at home he shuts himself up. He will not trouble himself about you at all. You will be given rooms and you will keep to them."

"Why should I want to trouble him?" said Mary, and she meant it as a rudeness, and Mrs. Medlock looked at her for a moment.

They drove six miles over the moor in the dark, and Mary could not see anything and could hear the wind.

"What is that noise? It is not the sea."

"It is the wind in the bushes on the moor," said Mrs. Medlock. "It is a wild, dreary enough place to my mind, though there is plenty that likes it — particularly when the heather is in bloom."

The house was very long and very dark and had a great many windows, and they went in through a hall with a fire in it that did not warm anything.

"You are to go to your room," said Mrs. Medlock. "It is up two flights and along a corridor and then along another one. You are not to wander about the house. Mr. Craven will not have it."

Mary's room had a fire and supper on a table, and a picture of a girl in a stiff dress on the wall, and one window.

She woke in the morning because a housemaid was on her knees at the grate cleaning it out, and the housemaid's name was Martha, and Martha talked.

This was the first surprise Mary had at Misselthwaite: a servant who talked to her.

"Are you going to be my servant?"

"I am Mrs. Medlock's servant," said Martha cheerfully, "and she is Mr. Craven's — but I am to do the housemaid's work up here and wait on you a bit. You will not need much waiting on."

"Who is going to dress me?"

Martha sat back on her heels and stared.

"Cannot you dress yourself?"

"No," said Mary. "I never did in my life. My Ayah dressed me."

"Well," said Martha, "it is time you learned. My mother always says she cannot see why grand people's children do not turn out fair fools, what with nurses and being washed and dressed and took out to walk like they was dogs."

Mary had never been spoken to like that in her life. She was so angry that she got out of bed and got her own clothes on, out of temper, with Martha handing her the shoes and telling her the buttons, and by the time it was done she had dressed herself for the first time.

Then she ate her breakfast, which was porridge, and did not like it, and Martha said that the children at home would have eaten the bowl.

"How many are there?"

"Twelve," said Martha, "on sixteen shillings a week. My brother Dickon is twelve and he has got a pony of his own that he tamed on the moor himself, and he is out on the moor from morning to night and the animals go to him."

Mary said nothing to that, but she thought about it.

"You had better go out," said Martha. "Get your coat and go out and play. There is nothing to do in the house and you cannot go anywhere in it."

"Out? In the rain?"

"It is not raining now," said Martha.

### Bölüm 3: The Robin

The gardens were behind the house: kitchen gardens and fruit gardens, one inside another, with walls round them and doors between them.

Mary walked through three of them and did not see a person, and then she came into one where an old man was digging.

He did not look up.

"What is this place?"

"One of the kitchen gardens."

"What is that?" said Mary, pointing at a green door in a wall.

"Another of them. There is another on t'other side of that, and an orchard on t'other side of that."

Mary went through the door and there was another wall, and another door, and behind the last one an orchard with grass and bare fruit trees.

And at the end of the orchard there was a wall, and the wall went round something, and there was ivy on it, and it had no door.

She walked the whole length of it twice.

There was no door. There was no gate. There was no opening at all, and the ivy was thick and old, and the tops of trees showed above it, and one of them had something on it — a red thing, moving.

It was a bird with a red breast, and it was sitting on the top of a tree inside the wall, and it began to sing.

Mary stood and listened to it, and something happened in her that she could not have described at nine years old, and I am not going to describe it either.

She went back to the man who was digging.

"There is a garden with no door."

The old man stopped digging.

"There was a door ten year ago," he said.

"Where is it?"

"It was buried," he said. "He had it buried."

"Why?"

"Mr. Craven had it shut when Mrs. Craven died so sudden. He locked the door and dug a hole and put the key in it. — That is Mrs. Medlock's bell; I must be off."

He went, and Mary stood by the wall.

She asked Martha about it that evening and Martha said the same thing in more words: the garden had been the mistress's; there had been a tree in it with a low branch she used to sit on; she had fallen from it and been carried in and died the next day; and Mr. Craven had locked the door and buried the key and would not let anybody speak of it.

"Ten years," said Mary.

"Ten years," said Martha.

For the next three days Mary went out every morning and walked the paths, and she walked round the outside of that wall every single day.

On the fourth morning the wind was hard and there was sun with it, and she went out and the robin was on the wall of the kitchen garden — the same bird; she was sure of it, because it flew ahead of her along the top of the wall and stopped when she stopped, three times.

She followed it down a walk she had not been down before, between two long walls, and it flew onto a bare bed of earth where a dog had been scratching, and hopped about there and looked at her with its head on one side.

Mary looked at the place the bird was standing on.

There was something in the loose earth: a ring of old iron.

She got down on her knees and pulled at it, and it came up out of the ground, and it was a key — an old key, long and rusty, that had been buried a long time and had been dug up by the wind and the rain and something with paws.

Mary sat back on her heels with the key in her hand.

The robin flew up onto the wall and sang four notes, and Mary did not say anything at all, and she put the key in her pocket and walked back to the house with her hand over the pocket the whole way.

### Bölüm 4: The Key

She had the key for a week and did not use it.

This is the part of the story that nobody expects, and it is the truest part of it: she carried that key in her pocket for seven days, and every day she went out and walked along the wall and put her hand on the ivy in a different place, and went in again.

She had a key and no keyhole.

On the eighth morning the wind came up hard again while she was standing there, and it blew the ivy out from the wall in a long swing, and Mary caught it with both hands and held it back.

Under it there was a square of green wood, and in the wood there was a round iron plate, and under the plate there was a hole.

She put the key in.

It was stiff, and she had to use two hands, and it turned.

Then she stood with her hand on it and did not push, and she looked over her shoulder along the walk to make sure nobody was coming, and there was nobody, and she pushed the door open slowly and slipped inside and shut it behind her, and stood with her back against it and breathed.

She was standing inside the secret garden.

It was the sweetest, most mysterious-looking place anybody could imagine, and it was also, to look at plainly, a wreck.

The walls were covered with the leafless stems of climbing roses which had grown so thick that they were matted together. There were rose trees that had grown into standards and were bare. There were grey stems going from tree to tree and hanging down in swings. There was grass everywhere, brown and short, and there were flower beds under the grass with nothing in them that Mary could see.

Nobody had touched any of it for ten years.

She walked round the whole of it once, on the grass, and it was quiet in a way that no other place she had been in was quiet, and there was one thing in it that she noticed and did not understand: the roses were grey and dry and there were a great many of them, and she could not tell whether they were dead.

She said it out loud, because there was nobody there.

"Are they all dead? Is it all dead?"

And then, because saying it made it worse, she got down on her knees in one of the beds and pulled some of the grass away with her hands.

The grass came away easily. Under it the earth was black and clear.

And in the earth, in a little circle where she had cleared it, there were pale green points sticking up.

She did not know what they were. She looked at them for some time and did not touch them, and then she cleared the grass away from the ones near them, and there were more — six, then nine, then a dozen — and she went along that bed on her knees pulling grass until she had cleared a space the size of a hearthrug.

Then she stood up.

"It is not a dead garden," she said. "Even if the roses are dead, there are other things alive."

She did not know how to garden. She had never dug anything and never planted anything and had never in her life been alone in a place that nobody else knew she was in.

She worked for two hours. She cleared four small spaces around four sets of green points, and she did it badly and pulled up two of them by accident and put them back, and by the end of it her hands were black and her legs ached and she was hungry.

She let herself out at the door and locked it and put the key in her pocket and pushed the ivy back over the plate.

At lunch Martha said she had never seen the child eat like that, and Mary said the wind on the moor made her hungry, and looked at her plate.

### Bölüm 5: Inside

For a week Mary went into the garden every morning and cleared grass.

She did it in the same order every day: in at the door, lock it behind her, key in her pocket, once round the whole garden, and then down on her knees where she had stopped the day before.

By the end of the week she had cleared four beds and could count what was in them.

On the Thursday she scraped at one of the grey rose stems near the wall with a piece of sharp wood, and under the dry outside of it there was green.

She sat back and looked at it.

Then she scraped six more, and four of them were green inside and two were not, and by the sixth she could tell the difference without cutting.

That afternoon she went to find the old gardener, who was called Ben Weatherstaff, and asked him a question she had worked out on the way so that it would give nothing away.

"If you have a rose, and it looks grey and dry, how do you know whether it is dead?"

Ben stopped digging and looked at her sideways.

"Cut it," he said. "If it is wick inside, it is not dead."

"Wick?"

"Alive," said Ben. "That is what we say here. Wick."

Mary said, "Thank you," and went away, and Ben Weatherstaff watched her go and said something to himself that she did not hear.

The next morning she took the scissors out of the housemaid's basket without asking and did what he had said, on twelve stems, and eight of them were wick.

She stood in the middle of the garden with the scissors in her hand.

"Eight," she said out loud. "Eight out of twelve."

And that morning she began the other thing — not clearing: cutting back. She cut the dead wood off the eight that were wick, and she did it badly, and cut too close on two of them, and by noon there was a heap of grey sticks on the grass beside her the size of a small bonfire.

She looked at the heap for a while.

Then she carried it in four loads to the far corner behind a tree, where nobody would see it if anybody ever came in, and came back and stood in a garden that had four cleared beds, eight cut-back roses and nothing in the middle of it.

It did not look like a garden. It looked like something that had been started.

At supper she asked Martha a question.

"If somebody wanted a spade — a little one — and some seeds, how much would they be?"

"A spade? What do you want a spade for?"

"To dig," said Mary. "There is nothing to do here. If I had a little spade I could make a garden somewhere."

Martha put down the bread.

"That is the first sensible thing I have heard out of your mouth," she said. "My mother said last week, 'That child has got to be out in the air and she has got to have something in her hands.' — Two shillings for a set of tools, and a penny a packet for seeds, and there is a man in the village who sells both. I will send the order with my brother Dickon and he will bring them up."

"Martha — I have money. Mrs. Medlock gave me some. Will you write the letter?"

"I will," said Martha. "And Dickon will pick the seeds himself, because he knows what will come up in this soil and what will not, and he will not charge you for knowing it."

The tools came four days later, left at the kitchen door in the morning with a packet of seeds and a piece of paper, and on the paper somebody had drawn, in pencil, a picture of a bird sitting in a nest.

Mary carried the whole lot out to the garden in her coat, and dug the first bed properly that afternoon with a spade that was the right size for her hands.

### Bölüm 6: Dickon

She met him a week later, in the wood outside the garden wall, and she heard him before she saw him: somebody playing four notes on a wooden pipe, over and over.

He was sitting under a tree with his back against it, a boy of twelve in patched clothes, with a round red face and very blue eyes, and there was a squirrel on the trunk beside his head, and two rabbits on the grass, and a bird on a branch two feet away, and not one of them moved when Mary came round the tree.

She stopped and did not move either.

"Do not stir," he said, without turning his head. "It would flight them. — I am Dickon. I know you are Miss Mary."

He put the pipe down slowly and the animals went off, unhurried, in three directions.

"I got your seeds," he said. "I have them here. — I put in some poppies and some larkspur because they come up easy and they do not want much, and there is a packet of mignonette, and it smells the best of the lot. Where is your garden?"

Mary went red. She had been thinking about this since the letter and had not decided.

"I have got a garden," she said, and then she said the whole of it in one breath: "It is a secret garden. It has been shut for ten years and nobody knows where it is and I found the key in the ground and I got in and nobody knows I go there but you — and if it is found out I shall lose it, and I do not know why I am telling you."

Dickon looked at her for a moment.

"I would keep a secret ten year," he said. "I keep them for the birds. I know where a bird's nest is that a man would pay money for. — Where is it?"

She took him along the walk and pushed the ivy back and unlocked the door, and they went in and she shut it behind them, and Dickon stood in the middle of that garden and turned round once, slowly, and looked at the whole of it.

"I never thought I would see this place," he said.

"You knew of it?"

"Martha told me about it when I was seven. We always knew there was one shut up." He went to the nearest wall and put his hand on a stem. "There will be roses all over here in June. Look at this — wick as you or me."

"I cut those back," said Mary. "I did not know how."

Dickon went along the eight of them, one at a time, and looked at what she had done, and Mary stood behind him and waited to be told.

"You cut two of them too close," he said. "They will live. — You did the rest right, and that is more than most does the first time. Who told you?"

"Nobody. I looked at them."

"Ay," said Dickon. "That is how you learn it."

They worked until the light went: he showed her how deep to dig and how far apart to put the seeds, and how to leave the wild things that ought to be left, and he stopped her twice from pulling something up.

"That is a young crocus. Leave it be."

"It looks like grass."

"It does," said Dickon, "and it is not, and in a month you will not be able to mistake them."

At the end of it they stood by the door and looked back at the garden.

"It ought not to be made tidy," said Dickon. "It would not be like itself. If it is a wild garden it should stay a bit wild."

"Do not let us make it tidy," said Mary.

He went out through the door first and she locked it behind them, and he said, coming down the walk, in the ordinary way: "I will come back Monday and I will bring my spade. — And I will not say a word about it to anybody, not even to my mother, because she does not ask what she is not told."

Mary walked back to the house and went up to her room, and at supper Martha said something to her twice before she heard it.

### Bölüm 7: Green Points

In March the weather turned and stayed turned for eleven days.

Mary was out from after breakfast until it was dark. She had stopped asking Martha whether she might go; she took her coat and went.

They worked in the garden four mornings a week — Dickon came when he could, and when he did not come she worked alone and did what he had shown her, and there was a rule between them, made without being discussed, that whoever came first did the watering.

The green points got taller.

Mary counted them. That was the thing she did and she did it every day, and by the middle of the month she had a number for each of the four beds, and she kept the numbers in her head and never wrote them down, and when the numbers went up she counted them again to be sure.

The first flower opened on the twentieth of March: a small yellow one, low down, in the bed nearest the wall.

She did not tell anybody about it. She looked at it in the morning and looked at it again before she left, and then she went in to lunch, and Mrs. Medlock, meeting her in the corridor, stopped and looked at her.

"You have got some colour in your face," said Mrs. Medlock. "You look a different child."

"I am hungry," said Mary, and went past her.

Here is the change in Mary Lennox that month, and I am going to put it in the way it actually happened, which is not the way people tell it afterwards.

She did not become a pleasant child. She was still short with Martha twice a week; she still disliked Mrs. Medlock; she still would not answer a question she thought was nosey.

What changed was measurable, and it was this: she could now walk the whole way round the outside wall without stopping, which she had not been able to do in January. She ate two helpings at every meal. She woke up before she was called. She had a set of tools she cleaned herself, and she could dig for an hour without her arms shaking, and she cared very much about eleven numbers in her own head.

One afternoon Ben Weatherstaff was standing at the top of a ladder against the orchard wall, cutting something, and he looked over the top of it while Mary was walking below.

"Springtime is coming," he said. "Cannot you smell it?"

Mary sniffed.

"I smell something fresh and damp."

"That is the good rich earth," said Ben. "It is in a good humour making ready to grow things. It is glad when planting time comes. — In the flower gardens out there things will be stirring down below in the dark. The sun is shining warm on them and you will see bits of green spikes sticking out of the black earth after a bit."

"What will they be?"

"Crocuses and snowdrops and daffodils. Have you never seen them?"

"No," said Mary. "Everything is hot and wet and green after the rains in India. I think things grow up in a night."

"These do not grow up in a night," said Ben Weatherstaff. "You will have to wait for them. They will grow a bit one day and a bit another. You watch them."

"I am going to," said Mary.

She went back to the garden and counted the bed by the wall again — fourteen, where it had been eleven at the beginning of the month — and then she sat down on the grass with her back against a tree, which she had not once done since she found the place, because until that day there had always been something to clear.

She stayed there until the light went.

### Bölüm 8: Crying in the Corridor

It rained for two days at the end of March and Mary could not go out.

On the first evening she heard it again.

She had heard it once in her first week and had been told it was the wind, and she had believed it because she had been new. Now she had lived in that house for two months and she knew what the wind on that moor sounded like, and this was not it.

It was a child crying, a long way off, inside the house.

She went out into the corridor with a candle.

The corridor had doors on both sides. She went along it and the sound got no nearer, and she came to a corner and turned it, and then there was a tapestry on a wall with a door behind it, half open, and the sound was coming through the door.

She pushed it and went along another passage with a carpet on the floor, and at the end of that one there was light under a door.

She opened it.

The room was large, with a fire, and furniture with carving on it, and a four-poster bed with curtains, and in the bed there was a boy.

He was about her own age. He had a thin white face and grey eyes that looked too big for it, and hair that fell over his forehead, and he had been crying and had stopped, because the door had opened.

They looked at each other for about four seconds.

"Who are you?" he said. "Are you a ghost?"

"No," said Mary. "Are you?"

"No. I am Colin. — Colin Craven."

"Craven?"

"Mr. Archibald Craven is my father."

"He is my uncle," said Mary. "I am Mary Lennox. Nobody ever told me he had a son."

"Come here," said Colin.

She came to the side of the bed and he put out his hand and touched her arm, as though he wanted to know whether she was there.

"You are real," he said. "I dream things sometimes."

And then, because he was a boy who had never in his life had anybody in that room who was not paid to be in it, he told her everything in about six minutes and did not once ask her a question.

He had been ill since he was born. His back was expected to grow crooked like his father's, and he was expected to die, and everybody in the house believed both of these things and so did he. His mother had died when he was born. His father could not bear to look at him, because of the eyes, which were his mother's. He had a doctor — a cousin — who would have Misselthwaite if Colin died, and who came every day and did what Colin told him. He had never been outside except twice, in a carriage, wrapped up, and the second time somebody had looked at him in the street and he had made them turn back.

He had servants who were dismissed if they let him be seen.

"Everybody has to do what I please," he said. "It makes me ill to be angry. — Nobody believes I shall live to grow up."

"Do you believe it?"

"I do not know," said Colin, and then, after a moment: "My mother's garden was shut up when she died. My father had the door locked and the key buried. — If I asked him to open it, he would not, and if I asked him for the key he would have the whole place dug up."

Mary sat on the edge of the bed with her hands in her lap.

She had a key in the pocket of her coat, which was hanging in her own room, four corridors away.

"Tell me about India," said Colin.

So she told him about India, and then about the moor, and then about Dickon and the pony and the animals in the wood, and he asked eleven questions about the animals in a row.

At about eleven o'clock a nurse came in and dropped a glass.

"How long has she been here?"

"She is my cousin," said Colin. "She is going to come and talk to me. You will not say anything about it to Mrs. Medlock, and if you do, I shall find out."

Mary went back to her own room along four corridors in the dark and got into bed and lay there for some time.

She did not tell him about the garden.

### Bölüm 9: Colin

She went to him every day for a week, in the afternoons when it rained and in the evenings when it did not, and Mrs. Medlock was told by the nurse that the young master was better and asked no further questions, because a house runs on not asking.

Colin talked. Mary listened for about four days and then stopped listening in the way she had been listening, and began to answer back.

The first quarrel was about the doctor.

"He says I am not to be crossed, because it makes me ill."

"You make yourself ill," said Mary. "You lay in that bed and think about it. — I know somebody who thinks about a bird's nest all day and he is never ill at all."

The second quarrel was worse and it was about the garden.

He had had the nurse bring him a book with pictures of gardens in it, and he said, in the middle of the afternoon, that he intended to have the shut-up garden dug up and opened while he was still alive, and that he would order it, and that they would have to do it because he was going to die anyway.

"If you order it," said Mary, "twenty men will come with spades and it will be full of people and it will not be a secret garden any more. It will be a garden that everybody has been in."

Colin lay still.

"How do you know what it would be?"

And Mary, who had been holding it for eight days, put both hands on the edge of the bed and said it.

"Because I have been in it."

He did not say anything for a moment.

"You have been in it? — How?"

"I found the key," said Mary. "The wind blew the ivy and I found the door. I have been going in every day since the beginning of March. Dickon comes. We have cleared four beds and cut back the roses and there are fourteen green points in the bed by the wall and one yellow flower came out on the twentieth of March. — And nobody knows but us, and now you."

Colin put his hands over his face.

"Mary. Could I —" and he stopped, and started again. "Do you think I could see it? Could I be taken there? Am I going to live long enough?"

And Mary said the thing that changed the whole of the rest of that book, and she said it in the flat voice she used for facts.

"Of course you are going to live long enough. Do not be silly."

Nobody in that house had ever said that to him.

The nurse came in ten minutes later and found the two of them looking at a garden book with their heads together, and she went out and told the doctor, and the doctor came the next day and looked at Colin and said that he seemed better and that it was surprising.

"It is the girl," said the nurse. "He eats when she is there."

The doctor — whose name was Craven and who would have Misselthwaite if the boy died — stood at the end of the bed and said that this was very good news, and he said it twice.

That week they made the plan, the three of them, with Dickon coming up the back stairs one afternoon in his boots and being introduced.

The plan had four parts and Colin insisted on all of them:

One, nobody was to be told. Two, Colin was to say that he wanted to be taken out in his chair for the air and that the gardeners were to be ordered off the paths so that nobody looked at him. Three, Dickon would push the chair. Four, when they were inside, the door would be shut and locked from the inside.

"And nobody is to know until I am ready," said Colin.

"Ready for what?" said Mary.

Colin did not answer that, and I am not going to answer it either, because he had worked something out in that bed and had not told either of them, and it took him five weeks.

### Bölüm 10: The Wheelchair

They did it on a morning at the end of April.

The order went down the house the evening before: the young master was going out in his chair, and no gardener and no servant was to be on any of the walks between ten and twelve, on his own instruction.

At ten o'clock two footmen carried him down the stairs and put him in the chair on the terrace with rugs over his knees, and went back inside, and the door was shut behind them.

Dickon took the handles.

They went along the terrace and down the walk between the two long walls, and Colin sat with his head back and looked at things: the sky, the top of a wall, a bird going over, a branch coming out of a tree.

He had been outside twice in his life.

He did not say anything at all for about eight minutes, and Mary walked beside the chair and did not say anything either.

At the ivy she went ahead and put her hand in and pushed the leaves back, and there was the plate and the hole, and she put the key in and turned it, and pushed the door open, and Dickon took the chair through the opening in one push, because the sill was low, and Mary shut the door behind them and locked it.

Colin put his hands up over his eyes and held them there.

Mary and Dickon stood still.

Then he took his hands down and looked round the garden — at the walls with the grey stems on them, and the beds, and the trees with green on them, and the grass, and the corner where the heap had been carried — and the first thing he said was not about any of it.

"I shall get well," he said. "I shall get well, and I shall live for ever and ever and ever."

They stayed until twelve.

He was pushed round the whole garden twice and then round it again slowly with stops, and they showed him things: the eight roses that were wick, the numbers in each bed, the yellow flower that had come out in March and was over now, the place where the seeds had gone in, the tree with the low branch.

Nobody told him about the branch. He looked at it for a while and did not ask.

At noon Dickon took the chair out and Mary locked the door, and they came up the walk and along the terrace, and the footmen came out and carried him up.

At lunch he ate everything on the tray.

And here is where the story becomes a thing done every day, and I am going to write the whole of the next five weeks in one paragraph, because that is what it was.

They went every morning it did not rain. Colin was carried down at ten and taken in at twelve. Inside the garden he was got out of the chair and put on a rug on the grass — which was Dickon's idea and which took three days of argument with the nurse to arrange, in the form of an order from Colin that his rugs were to be sent down. He sat on the grass and dug with a trowel at the level of his own hands. He was given jobs. He kept the numbers for two of the four beds and Mary kept the other two, and they compared them at the end of the morning.

And twice a week Dickon's mother sent up, by Dickon, a can of new milk and a bag of currant buns, and the three of them ate them on the grass under the tree, and Colin ate his share and asked for more of it the second week, and this was talked about in the kitchen at Misselthwaite for a month because the young master had never in ten years asked for food twice.

What nobody in the house knew, and what the doctor did not know when he came and said Colin was better and looked at the boy's arms and said something about the air, was the other thing that was going on in that garden every morning at about half past eleven.

### Bölüm 11: Standing

It began on the ninth morning, and it was Colin's idea, and he had had it in bed in April and had not told anybody.

He wanted to stand up.

Not to walk: to stand. He had never stood on the ground without two people holding him, and he had been told all his life that his back was going to be crooked and that his legs were no good, and he had worked out in bed that neither of these things had ever been tested.

So on the ninth morning he said, sitting on the rug, "Dickon. I am going to stand on my feet, and you are going to hold me under the arms until I do it, and Mary is going to stand in front where I can see her, and neither of you is going to say anything about it afterwards to anybody."

Dickon got him up.

He stood for about half a minute, holding Dickon's arm, on the grass in the middle of the garden, and his legs shook the whole time and he was extremely white, and he did not fall.

Then he sat down.

"Do you see?" he said. "I can stand."

"You can," said Dickon.

Mary did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that would not have spoiled it.

They did it every morning after that, and the second week he stood without holding on, and the third week he took four steps with Dickon beside him and no hand on him, and by the middle of June he could walk the whole way round that garden and stop twice.

And here is the arrangement they made, and it was Colin's and it lasted six weeks.

Nobody in the house was to know.

He was carried down at ten as usual. He was carried up at twelve as usual. He was pushed in the chair along the terrace where the windows could see him, and he lay back in it with the rugs over his knees looking exactly as ill as he had in April, and he complained about the jolting for the benefit of the footmen, and once he told the nurse that he was worse and she wrote it down.

Mary asked him why, in the garden, in the third week.

"Because when my father comes back," said Colin, "I want to walk to him."

They were found out once. Ben Weatherstaff put his head over the top of the orchard wall on a ladder in the second week and looked into that garden and saw three children in it and one of them standing up on the grass — and he did not shout, and he did not go to Mrs. Medlock. He came down off the ladder and came round to the door and knocked on it, and was let in, and stood inside it with his cap in his hands and looked at Colin for some time.

"They said you was a cripple," said Ben Weatherstaff.

"I am not," said Colin.

"No," said Ben. "You are not."

And then — because he had been the gardener there when Colin's mother was alive, and because he was seventy, and because he had gone over that wall on a ladder twice a year for ten years to prune the roses so that they would not die, which nobody in that house had known and which came out in that garden on that morning — he sat down on a barrow and told them which roses had been the mistress's, and then he got up and went back to work, and he came into that garden every week after that and did not say a word about it to anybody.

### Bölüm 12: The Walk Back

Archibald Craven was abroad that summer and had been abroad for four months, and he had had a letter in June from Dickon's mother, and it was four lines long and said one thing: that she would be glad if he would come home.

He came in the last week of July.

He had not been in that house in the summer for ten years. He went in through the hall and asked for Mrs. Medlock and asked after the boy, and was told that the young master was out in the gardens in his chair, and that he was out in them every day now, and that Mrs. Medlock did not understand it and that the doctor did not either.

He went out and along the terrace, and he did not know why he was going that way, and then he went down the walk between the two long walls, and he stopped at the place where the ivy was.

He had not stood there for ten years.

And while he was standing there he heard something on the other side of the wall, which was the last thing anybody would hear in a garden that had been locked for ten years: running feet, and voices, and a boy laughing.

Then the door came open in the ivy in front of him — a door he had buried the key of — and a boy came out of it at a run, going too fast to stop, and ran into him.

Archibald Craven put out both hands to keep him off, and held him at arm's length, and looked at him.

The boy was tall for twelve, his face was brown, his hair was in his eyes, and he was breathing hard, and he had grey eyes that were his mother's, and he was standing on his own two legs on the gravel.

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

"Colin?"

"Yes," said Colin. "It is Colin. — I was running a race with Mary and Dickon and I won it. I did not know you were coming today."

"In the garden," said his father. "In the garden."

"Yes," said Colin. "Mary found the key and got in. — It is alive. Will you come in and see it? Do not have it dug up. Come in and sit down."

They went in, and the man sat down on the seat under the tree where the low branch was, and he did not say anything for a long time, and Mary and Dickon went to the far end of the garden and worked with their backs turned, which nobody had told them to do.

Colin sat beside his father and told him the whole of it in order: the key in the ground, the ivy in the wind, the four beds, the roses that were wick, the eight numbers, Dickon, the milk and the buns, the rug on the grass, and the standing up.

"You have been walking six weeks."

"Yes."

"And nobody in the house knows it."

"No," said Colin. "I kept it because I wanted to walk to you."

His father put his hand over his eyes and held it there for a while.

Then he said, "Show me the roses," and Colin took him round the garden and showed him, and told him which ones Ben Weatherstaff had been coming over the wall to prune, and his father stopped at that one and stood looking at it.

They went in to the house at six o'clock across the lawns.

And this is the last picture in the book and I want it exactly right, because it was seen by the whole of that household from the windows and was talked about in that kitchen for twenty years.

Mrs. Medlock was in the hall. The nurse was at a window on the first floor. Martha was on the stairs with a tray she had stopped carrying. Ben Weatherstaff was at the corner of the terrace with a spade, and he did not pretend to be working.

Across the lawn, in the evening light, came Mr. Craven with his shoulders back, which nobody in that house had seen; and beside him, not carried, not in a chair, not held by anybody, walking as strongly as any boy in that county, came the young master of Misselthwaite.

### Bölüm 1: Spring Cleaning

The Mole had been cleaning his house all morning.

He had brushes in both hands and dust on his black fur and a smear of whitewash on his nose, and above him, through the earth of the ceiling, something was going on that he could not see and did not like to miss.

He put the brush down.

"Bother," said the Mole. "Bother spring cleaning."

He went up his tunnel, scraping and scrabbling with his small strong paws, and came out into the sunshine and lay on the warm grass and did nothing at all for some time.

The field was full of the smell of things growing. He rolled in it. Then he walked, because a mole who has been indoors since November walks first and thinks afterwards, and he went along a hedge and under a gate and across a meadow, and then he stopped, because in front of him there was a great deal of water.

The Mole had never seen a river before.

It was wide and brown and it moved, and it made a sound all the time, and the sound did not stop while he stood there and did not stop after he sat down. He sat on the bank for a long while with his paws in his lap and watched the water carry things away.

On the far side there was a hole in the bank, just above the waterline, and in the hole there was a small brown face with whiskers.

"Hullo, Mole," said the Water Rat.

"Hullo, Rat."

"Would you like to come over?"

"I should like it very much," said the Mole, "but there is the water."

The Rat untied a small blue and white boat and came across with two oars, and the Mole got in very carefully, holding both sides, and sat down where he was told.

"This is the first boat I have ever been in," said the Mole.

"What?" said the Rat. "Never been in a — you have never — " and he stopped rowing, and then began again, and said, "Then we shall spend the whole day in one. I shall get a basket."

The basket, when it came out of the hole, was large, and the Mole watched it being packed and asked what was in it.

"Coldchickenbreadbutterhamcressandwichespicklesgingerbeer — "

"Stop, stop," said the Mole. "This is too much."

"Do you think so?" said the Rat. "It is what I always take, and I have often thought it rather little."

They went up the river all afternoon. The Rat rowed and the Mole sat and looked at everything: the reeds, the ducks upside down in the shallows, the willow trailing in the water, a cow drinking with its feet in the mud. Nobody said very much. The river said the same thing all afternoon, and neither of them wanted it to stop.

They ate the basket on the grass by a weir.

"And beyond the wood?" said the Mole, when they had finished, pointing at the dark line on the horizon.

"That is the Wild Wood," said the Rat. "We do not go there much."

"Are the people in it — are they not nice?"

"The squirrels are all right, and the rabbits, some of them. Badger lives in the middle and nobody troubles him. Then there are the weasels and the stoats and the foxes." The Rat lay back with his hat over his eyes. "They break out sometimes. It is not the sort of thing one talks about at tea."

"And beyond the Wild Wood?"

"Beyond the Wild Wood is the Wide World," said the Rat, "and that is something that does not matter, either to you or to me. I have never been there and I am never going, and neither are you, if you have any sense. Do not mention it again."

The Mole, who was full of ham and gingerbeer and had been in a boat, thought that he had never met anybody so entirely right about everything.

Then he asked whether he might row.

### Bölüm 2: The Picnic

Rowing is not a thing that can be learned in an afternoon, and the Mole had been in the boat about four hours.

"You must let me try," he said. "It looks so easy."

"Not yet," said the Rat. "Not today."

The Mole took the oars anyway, when the Rat got up to look at something in the reeds, and he took them suddenly, and the Rat sat down again in a hurry.

"Now then," said the Mole.

He put both oars into the water at once, very deep, and pulled with all his strength, and the oars did not come out where he expected them to, and his legs went up over his head, and he found himself lying on the top of the Rat at the bottom of the boat with the sky where the water ought to be. Then the boat went over.

The river was very cold and the Mole went under it twice.

Something took hold of the back of his neck and towed him to the bank, and he came out with weed in his ears and sat down.

The Rat dived for the oars, and then for the hat and the basket, and finally for the boat, and swam it in and tipped the water out of it, and did all this without saying anything at all, which the Mole found much worse than being shouted at.

"Ratty," said the Mole at last, "I am very sorry. Will you forgive me? I have been a stupid animal and I have nearly lost your boat and everything in it."

"That is all right," said the Rat cheerfully. "What is a little wet to a water rat? I am in the water most days. Do not think about it any more. And look here — you had better come and stay with me for a while. My house is plain, but I will teach you to row and to swim, and you shall be as much at home on the water as any of us."

The Mole was so touched that he could not find any words, and had to wipe his eye with the back of his paw.

They walked home along the towpath with the boat on a rope and had a hot dinner and the Mole sat by the fire in a borrowed dressing gown while his own clothes dried, and the Rat told stories about the river all evening: about the herons, and the barges, and the great flood of some year before, and the family of otters, and the eel who had views about everything.

Then the Mole was shown to the best bedroom, which looked out on the river, and the river talked all night in his ears, and he slept in the middle of it.

He stayed all summer.

He learned to swim in June and to row in July, and by August he could take the boat out by himself, though the Rat still watched from the bank when he thought the Mole was not looking. He learned the names of things: the reeds, the willows, the flag irons, the water lilies that shut in the evening. He learned that the river is never the same two weeks together and that everybody who lives on it says so, every year, as if it had never been said before.

One morning at the beginning of the summer he was sitting on the bank while the Rat sang a song about ducks, and he asked whether he might be introduced to the friend they were always passing — the one with the big white house behind the willows and the boathouse with three boats in it and none of them out.

"That is Toad Hall," said the Rat, "and Toad is the best of animals: simple, good-natured, affectionate. Perhaps not very clever. And he is always taking up something new, and being perfectly sure about it, and dropping it the week after."

"He sounds a nice animal."

"He is the nicest," said the Rat. "We will go up this morning. He will be pleased. He is always pleased, and he is always in the middle of something."

They got into the boat.

### Bölüm 3: The Caravan

Toad was on the lawn in front of Toad Hall, walking round something with his hands behind his back.

"Ratty!" he shouted. "Mole! You are the very animals I wanted. Come and look at this."

In the yard stood a caravan, canary yellow, picked out in green and red, with a door at the back and steps and small windows with curtains.

"There she is," said Toad. "The real life. The open road, the dusty highway, the heath, the hedgerows, the rolling downs! Villages, towns, cities! Here today, up and off to somewhere else tomorrow! Travel, change, interest, excitement!"

"Boats," said the Rat.

"Boats are finished," said Toad. "I gave up boats on Tuesday. Boats are a waste of time — I say it kindly, Ratty, but somebody must say it. Come inside and see how she is fitted."

She was very well fitted. There were bunks, a little table that folded up, a stove, lockers, bookshelves, a bird cage with a bird in it, and pots and pans and jugs and kettles of every size.

"All complete," said Toad, opening a locker. "Biscuits, potted lobster, sardines, soda water, tinned tongue, cards, dominoes. You will find everything you can possibly want."

"I am not coming," said the Rat. "I am stopping on my river, and I am going to live in a hole, and boat, as I always have."

Toad looked at him and then looked at the Mole, and the Mole, who wanted very much to go and did not want to say so, looked at the ground; and the Rat saw the whole of it and said nothing for a moment.

"Come along," said Toad to the Mole. "You shall drive the horse."

"Oh, very well," said the Rat. "We will start today."

They set out after lunch: the old grey horse in the shafts, complaining; Toad on the front step talking; the Mole leading the horse and being talked to; the Rat walking behind with his hands in his pockets.

It was a golden afternoon. They went along dusty lanes with birds in the hedges and men in the fields who stopped work to look at them, and they camped at dusk in a field a long way from anywhere, and had supper, and Toad told them what he was going to do next year and the year after.

The second day was the same and just as good, and the Mole thought that Toad had perhaps been right about the open road, and said so, and the Rat said, "Perhaps," and looked at the horse's hoof.

On the third day they came out onto the high road.

They were going along quietly, and the Mole and the Rat were walking on either side of the horse, when they heard a sound behind them, very faint, like a bee.

Then it was not like a bee.

Something came up the road behind them at a tremendous rate, filling the whole road with dust and noise — an enormous car, with a driver in goggles and a great many gentlemen in caps, and it went by them so close that the wind of it took the Mole's hat off, and it said *Poop-poop!* twice, and was a small cloud a long way off before anybody had got their breath.

The horse stood up on its back legs, went sideways, and put the caravan into the ditch, where it lay on its side with the pots coming out of the lockers and the bird cage on the grass and one wheel in the air.

The Rat got the bird cage right way up. The Mole held the horse. It took the two of them an hour to get the horse out of the harness and the harness out of the hedge, and they found that the caravan could not be moved without a man and a cart, and they left it, and walked five miles to the nearest village with the horse behind them.

Toad walked in the middle of the road all the way, and he had not said one word about the caravan.

"Poop-poop," said Toad dreamily, at the second milestone.

"What did you say?" said the Rat.

"Nothing," said Toad.

### Bölüm 4: The Wild Wood

The Mole wanted to meet the Badger.

He mentioned it in July and again in September, and the Rat said that Badger would turn up one day, and that Badger hated society and invitations and dinners and all that sort of thing, and that one did not call on Badger.

"Could we not go and call on him?"

"He lives in the middle of the Wild Wood," said the Rat, "and that is not the same as living down the lane. Wait till the winter. He will come."

The winter came. The river ran full and brown and the bank was cold, and the Rat slept a great deal, as animals do, and wrote poetry in the afternoons and dozed over it.

One afternoon, while the Rat was asleep in his chair, the Mole took his stick and went out.

The country was bare and grey and quiet. He liked it. There was nothing to hide anything: he could see the shape of the fields under the grass and the whole plan of the land, and he walked towards the dark line of the Wild Wood in high spirits, thinking that he would just look in at the edge of it.

Inside the wood the light went at once.

It was not unpleasant at first. There were sticks under his feet and fungus on the stumps like something in a story, and the trunks went up and away. Then the holes began.

He saw a face in a hole; only for a second; a little narrow face with hard eyes, and it was gone. He told himself it was nothing and went on. Then there were faces in a great many holes, on both sides, all looking at him, and all going when he looked back.

Then the whistling began, very low, behind him and then in front.

Then the pattering. It was small and light and it was everywhere in the dead leaves, and it came nearer, and it was following him, or coming to meet him, and he could not tell which.

The Mole ran.

He ran off the path and among the trunks and fell twice, and at last he got into a hollow under the roots of an old beech tree and pushed himself in as far as he would go, and lay there with his heart going, and outside the whistling and the pattering went on for a long while and then stopped, and he did not come out, because he had understood something about the Wild Wood that the Rat had never said in words and had certainly meant.

At the Rat's house, the fire burned down and the Rat woke up.

"Moly?"

The house was empty and the Mole's cap and stick were gone from the hall. The Rat went out to the bank and looked at the mud, and found small footprints going away from the river towards the wood, and came back into the house and took a pistol out of a drawer and a stout stick from the corner, and went out again at a trot.

It was nearly dark in the wood. He went along the paths calling, "Moly! Moly! Moly!" and stopping to listen, and it took him an hour, and the wood was full of small noises that stopped whenever he stopped.

At last, from a hollow in the roots of a beech, something answered him.

"Ratty. Is it really you?"

"O, Mole," said the Rat, sitting down on the leaves. "You should not have. I told you not to."

"You did not tell me why."

"No," said the Rat. "That is quite true. There are things we do not talk about at tea, and then somebody walks into them." He put his stick down. "Are you hurt?"

"Not hurt. Frightened." The Mole came out of the hole slowly. "Ratty, they were all round me."

"They generally are," said the Rat. "They do not come near a stick and a pistol, and they know me. Sit still a minute and get your breath, and then we shall go home."

They sat in the roots while the Mole's paws stopped shaking, and then they got up, and it was at that moment that the snow began.

It came down thick, without wind, and in ten minutes the wood was white and the paths were not where they had been, and the two of them walked and turned and walked, and the Rat, who had been in the Wild Wood a hundred times, stopped in the middle of a clearing that he had never seen before in his life and did not say so.

### Bölüm 5: Badger's Kitchen

They walked in the snow for an hour and got nowhere, and then the Mole, who was in front, tripped over something and went down on his face and said that his shin was cut.

The Rat scraped the snow away with his paws to see what had done it.

It was not a stone or a root. It was iron: a boot-scraper, the sort that stands outside a door.

"A boot-scraper," said the Mole. "In the middle of a wood. What good is that to us? We want to get home."

"Dig," said the Rat.

They dug. Under the snow was a doormat.

"A doormat," said the Mole. "Ratty, do come away. There is no sense in it."

"Dig," said the Rat again, and they dug where the mat had been lying, along the bank behind it, and they came to something hard and green, and they cleared it, and it was a door, with a bell handle beside it and a small brass plate under the bell, and on the plate, in neat letters, MR BADGER.

The Mole sat down in the snow.

"You are a clever animal," he said, "and I am a stupid one."

"Ring," said the Rat, "and pull hard. I am tired."

They rang for a long time. Then there was a slow shuffling inside, and bolts, and the door opened a few inches, and a long grey nose came out.

"Whoever it is at this hour," said the Badger, "if it is not something serious — "

"Badger, it is us. In the snow."

"Ratty, my dear man," said the Badger, and had them both inside in a moment and the door shut and the bolts across.

The hall was long and low and warm. The Badger put on his slippers and took them down a passage into a kitchen with a fire at one end, a long table with benches, hams hanging from the beams, baskets of eggs, and a settle where two animals might sit and be entirely out of the wind.

He made them sit down. He brought bread and cold ham and pickles and a bowl of something hot, and did not ask them anything at all until they had eaten, and he put dry things in front of the fire without being asked and moved the boots to where the heat was.

"Now," said the Badger, when the plates were empty. "How are things along the river?"

"Much the same," said the Rat. "The Otters have a new baby. And Toad — "

"Ah," said the Badger. "Toad."

"Toad had a caravan," said the Mole. "It went into a ditch."

"Then that is over, and there will be something else," said the Badger. "It is always something else. What is it now?"

"Motor-cars," said the Rat. "He has bought one. He had one week of the caravan and now he talks of nothing else. He has been out in it three times and he has smashed it twice."

"Three times," said the Badger. "I heard the third from the rabbits."

"And he will kill himself," said the Rat, "or somebody else, and there will be an end of Toad Hall, and his father was a friend of yours."

The Badger looked at the fire for a while.

"Nothing can be done at this season," he said. "We do not begin things in the winter, any of us; we sleep, and we mend what is indoors, and we wait. In the spring, when we are all in our own again and up early, we will take Toad seriously in hand — you and I and Mole — and we will bring him to reason, if it takes all day."

"In the spring," said the Rat.

"In the spring," said the Badger, and knocked out his pipe, and said that the beds were made up and that it was late.

They slept until nearly noon.

In the morning there were two young hedgehogs in the kitchen eating porridge, who had been on their way to school and had got lost in the snow; and Otter came in through the back passage, having tracked them from the river, and said he had never seen such a night; and the kitchen filled up, and there was a great deal of breakfast, and everybody talked at once.

The Badger showed them the way home in the afternoon through a passage that went under the wood and came out in a hollow near the edge of it, so that they walked in daylight and in the open the whole way back, and the field was white and quiet, and the river when they got to it was running as usual.

### Bölüm 6: The Motor-Car

The Badger came for Toad on a fine morning at the beginning of spring, and the three of them walked up to Toad Hall together.

A new car stood in front of the door, very large and very red, and Toad came down the steps in goggles, a cap, huge gloves and an enormous coat, pulling one glove on.

"Hullo!" he said. "You are just in time. You shall all come for a — "

"Take those things off him," said the Badger.

The Rat took one arm and the Mole the other and they had the coat and the goggles and the cap off Toad in half a minute, in the middle of the drive, while he said that this was not the way to treat a gentleman in his own house. Then the Badger took him by the arm and walked him into the smoking-room and shut the door, and the Rat and the Mole sat outside on two chairs and heard the Badger's voice going on steadily for three quarters of an hour, with Toad's coming in now and then, higher each time, and then not at all.

At last the door opened. The Badger came out leading Toad by the paw, and Toad's face was wet and his knees were not steady.

"He has seen the folly of it," said the Badger. "He has given me his word. Toad, tell them."

"I have been a fool," said Toad. "I see it now. There will be no more cars."

"You give up motor-cars entirely and for ever?"

Toad thought.

"Not entirely," he said. "Not for ever. I mean — "

The Badger took him back into the smoking-room and shut the door again, and this time it was twenty minutes, and this time when they came out Toad said what was wanted, word for word, and said it three times.

"Good," said the Badger. "Now, we three are going to stay in this house until you are cured, and one of us will be with you all day. Ratty first."

Toad went upstairs to lie down, being unwell, and the Rat went up with him and sat by the window.

Half an hour later there was a groan from the bed.

"Ratty. I am very ill. Fetch a doctor — and while you are out, a lawyer. There are things I must arrange."

The Rat looked at him and thought that Toad's colour was not good, and that Toad had never in his life been ill for more than an hour, and that a lawyer was a strange thing to ask for, and that if he were wrong about it he should not forgive himself; and he went for the doctor.

Toad got out of bed as soon as the front door shut, knotted the sheets, went out of the window, and was in the road in four minutes with his hands in his pockets, whistling.

He had his lunch at the Red Lion in the next town, at a table by the window, and he was in the middle of it when a car came into the yard and a party of gentlemen got out and came in talking about the state of the roads.

Toad paid his bill and went out into the yard.

The car stood there with nobody in it.

He walked round it once, slowly, in the way he had walked round the caravan.

"I wonder," said Toad, "whether this sort of car starts easily."

He was in the seat, and then he was out of the yard, and then he was on the high road with the dust going up behind him, and he did not know how much of the road he covered or how fast, and he heard himself saying that he was Toad, the great Toad, the Toad who had never been beaten yet, and he was still saying it when the constable stepped out into the road with his hand up.

They took him before the magistrates the same afternoon.

The clerk read the charges: stealing a valuable motor-car; driving to the public danger; and gross impertinence to the rural police.

"Twelve months for the theft," said the Chairman, "three years for the driving, and fifteen for the cheek to the police, which is the worst of the three. Fifteen and three and twelve — nineteen, or is it twenty? Say twenty years, and let us have no more trouble."

They put the irons on him and took him away through the town, past the market and the shops and the people, and down a lane to a very old castle with a moat, and in through the gate, and down and down, past guards with pikes, to a small stone room in the middle of the keep with one window high up; and the door was shut and the key was turned.

And there they left the most miserable toad in England.

### Bölüm 7: The Prison

Toad lay on the floor of the stone room and gave himself up.

He said aloud that this was the end of everything; that the world had lost a fine animal; that they would put up a statue to him when it was too late; and he said all of this at some length, and there was nobody in the passage to hear it.

He stopped eating for two days. On the third day he ate everything on the tray and asked whether there was more.

The gaoler had a daughter, and she looked after the birds and the cats in the castle and had a great deal of feeling for animals in cages. She noticed the toad, and asked her father if she might take him his food, and her father said she might, because the man liked a quiet life.

She came in with a tray on the fourth day.

"Cheer up, Toad."

"Thank you," said Toad. "Do not mind me. I am a toad of no consequence. I have been the friend of dukes. Nothing matters now."

"There is bubble-and-squeak," said the girl, "and it is hot."

Toad sat up and ate it.

After that she came every day, and Toad talked. He told her about Toad Hall and the boathouse and the banqueting hall and the stables, and about the river, and about his friends — the Water Rat who was a good fellow and did not understand cars, the Mole who was a modest animal, and the Badger, who was, said Toad, a person of great weight in that part of the country and would certainly be doing something about all this.

The girl liked hearing about the river. She had never seen one.

"Toad," she said one morning, "I have an aunt who is a washerwoman."

"There, there. Never mind. I have several aunts who ought to be washerwomen."

"Do be quiet a minute. My aunt washes for the prisoners in this castle. She takes the linen out on Monday and brings it back on Friday, and today is Thursday. — She is about your figure."

"I am a handsome toad. She is a washerwoman."

"So is she a handsome washerwoman, in her way," said the girl. "I am offering to speak to her. She would give you her gown and bonnet for the money you have in your waistcoat, and you would walk out of the gate on Friday morning with the basket, and nobody looks at a washerwoman twice."

Toad considered this from the point of view of his own dignity for some time, and then considered the stone room, and then took the second view.

On the Friday morning the aunt came up and was paid, and gave over the gown, the apron, the shawl and the black bonnet, and was tied up loosely with a piece of rope by her own request, so that afterwards nobody could say she had helped, and put in the corner with the sheets over her.

Toad put on the gown. It fitted him better than he had hoped, which he did not mention.

He went out with the basket on his arm.

The first guard at the head of the stairs said something to him about the weather. Toad said, "Ah, well," in a high voice, and went on. The second guard said something else, and Toad said it again. At the gate a sergeant looked at him and said, "Now then, mother, you are early," and Toad said, "Ah, well," a third time, and the wicket was opened, and he was in the lane outside with the wall behind him and the basket on his arm, and it was raining a little.

He walked to the end of the lane before he began to run.

At the railway station he asked for a ticket to the village nearest Toad Hall, and put his paw where his waistcoat pocket had been, and found that he had no waistcoat and no pocket and no money at all.

The clerk said that in that case there would be no ticket, and that if the good woman would step out of the way there were people behind her.

Toad stood on the platform in the gown and the bonnet with the empty basket, and the train came in, and he watched it, and he cried — a washerwoman crying on a platform is a thing nobody looks at either — and then he walked along to the front and stood by the engine, and told the engine-driver the truth, most of it, in the wrong order.

The driver looked at him for a while.

"You are not a washerwoman."

"No."

"And you have no money."

"No."

"Well," said the driver, "I hate a policeman on my line, and I am going that way. Get up on the engine and wash my shirts when we get there, and say no more about it."

### Bölüm 8: The Piper at the Gate

While Toad was in prison there was a summer on the river, and one evening in that summer a young otter went missing.

His father came up the bank at dusk and stood outside the Rat's door and did not come in.

"He has been gone since the morning. He is small and he cannot swim well in the weir pool, and his mother is at home and I have said I am going to look for him and I have looked everywhere I can think of."

"Where would he go?" said the Rat.

"He has been talking for a week about the weir," said the otter. "About the ford at the weir. He has never seen it and he was told he was too small."

The Rat and the Mole took the boat out at eleven o'clock at night and rowed up.

It was one of those nights in June when it does not get properly dark. The reeds stood still. Nothing on that river made any noise for two hours except the water going over the weir, a long way ahead, always the same.

They rowed, and stopped, and called, and rowed again.

At about three o'clock the Rat stopped rowing and sat with the oars up.

"Mole. Can you hear something?"

"No."

"Listen. — There. It is like music, or like the reeds, or — no." He put his head on one side. "It is beautiful," he said, "and it is going that way," and he took the oars and rowed toward it, and the Mole could not hear anything at all and said so twice and then stopped saying it.

They came, at about four, to a small island in the middle of the river, above the weir, with willows on it and a bank of grass.

The Rat brought the boat in and got out without a word and went up the bank, and the Mole came after him, and at the top the Rat stood quite still.

What was on the island was this: a hollow in the grass, and in the hollow, asleep on his side with his paws together, the young otter, unhurt.

They stood there for a while. The Mole looked at the sleeping animal and then looked at the Rat, who was looking at something else, and who did not turn round, and whose whiskers were wet.

"Ratty. Are you all right?"

"Yes," said the Rat. "Give me a minute."

The light came up over the field on the far bank. The first bird started, and then all the others, in the way they do, all at once and far too loud.

And the Rat put his paw over his eyes and said, "I have forgotten it," and then, after a moment, "It is gone. — What was I saying?"

"You said there was music."

"Did I? Well, there is the weir. It is a trick of the water. — Wake him gently; he will be stiff."

They woke the young otter, who was surprised to see them and not at all frightened, and said that he had come to see the ford and had waited a long time for somebody to come and had gone to sleep.

They put him in the boat between them and rowed him home in the early light, and his father came down the bank to the water, and nobody in that boat said one word about the island.

The Rat did not go up the river again for a fortnight, and when the Mole asked him about it he said the fishing was better lower down, which was not true and was allowed to stand.

### Bölüm 9: The Open Road

In September the swallows began to talk about going, and the Rat became difficult to sit with.

He was short in his answers. He went out and came back and could not settle to anything, and he stood on the bank in the afternoons looking downstream, which was not a thing he had ever done before, because everything the Rat cared about was upstream or in the water in front of him.

One afternoon a stranger came along the towpath: a sea rat, thin, grey, with a ring in his ear and a small bundle, and he sat down on the grass beside the Rat as if the place had been kept for him.

He was given lunch, and he talked for three hours.

He talked about a harbour town in the south with white houses and a market full of things nobody on that river had ever eaten; about the winter he had spent in a warm country where the wine was cheap and the mules wore bells; about a ship's galley in a storm, and about a night in a Sicilian orchard where he had slept under a tree and been woken by bells; and he did not once say that any of it was better than a river, which was the cleverest thing about him.

The Rat sat with his chin in his paws and did not interrupt.

At about four the sea rat got up, said that he was going down to the coast to find a ship, and that the road went south from the top of the field, and that any animal with legs might take it.

And he went off down the towpath, and the Rat sat where he was for some minutes.

Then he got up, and went into his house, and came out with his stick and a small bundle, and started up the field toward the road with his eyes fixed and his mouth a straight line.

The Mole came out of the boathouse and saw the Rat's face and understood in about a second, and got in front of him.

"Ratty. Where are you going?"

"South," said the Rat. "With the others. To the sea. Let me by, Mole."

"You cannot walk to the sea in a September afternoon."

"Let me by."

The Mole did not let him by. He took hold of him, and there was a short struggle on the grass, and it was not funny and I am not going to make it funny; the Rat was not himself, and the Mole was frightened of him and held on anyway.

He got him back into the house and put him on the floor of the front room and held the door shut and did not let him out.

The Rat lay there for a long while with his eyes open, saying nothing. Then he shut them, and then he shivered once, and then he began to come back — slowly, the way a person comes back from a faint, in pieces.

The Mole waited until the eyes looked at him properly, and then went out quietly and came back with two things: the pencil and the paper on which the Rat wrote his verses, which he had not touched for a month.

He put them on the table without a word and went away to the other side of the room and made a great deal of noise with the fire irons.

After a while there was the sound of a pencil.

The Rat wrote until supper, and crossed out most of it, and said at supper that the poetry was bad and that he intended to keep at it, and asked whether there was any of the cold pie left.

The swallows went at the end of the month.

The Rat stood on the bank and watched them go, with his paws behind his back, and then went in and shut the door, and neither of them mentioned the road at the top of the field that autumn or ever.

### Bölüm 10: The Washerwoman

Toad washed two shirts on the engine, badly, and was put down at a station twenty miles from home at six in the morning, still in the gown and bonnet.

He walked. At the second village he came to a canal with a barge on it, and a large woman steering it, and she called out to him across the water.

"Nice morning, ma'am!"

"I dare say it is," said Toad, "to them that are not in my sad situation. I am a poor washerwoman and I have lost all my money and I cannot find my married daughter."

"Get on board," said the woman. "I am going your way and I have a quantity of washing myself. You shall do it in the cabin and I shall give you your dinner and your fare."

Toad got on the barge.

He was taken into the cabin and shown a tub, a bar of soap, and a heap of shirts.

He had been watching washerwomen from carriage windows all his life and had formed the impression that the whole business was a matter of putting things in water and being cheerful. He tried it for twenty minutes. The soap got everywhere except onto the shirts; the water went up his sleeves; a thing he had wrung out came apart at the shoulder.

The woman looked in at the door.

"You have never washed so much as a dish-cloth in your life."

"I am a well-known washerwoman," said Toad, "and I wash for three families —"

"You are a nasty crawling toad," said the woman, "and I have known it since you got on, and I only wanted to see how long you would keep it up."

And she took him by the fore-leg and the hind-leg and put him over the side into the canal, and laughed while she was doing it, which Toad said afterwards was the worst part.

He came out on the bank, wet through, with the gown round his knees, and ran along the towpath after the barge until he came up level with the horse.

Then he did the thing that finished the day.

He untied the horse from the barge, got up on it, and rode off across the fields with the bargewoman shouting behind him, and he sold the horse an hour later to a gipsy in a lane for six shillings and a breakfast, which was less than the horse was worth and more than Toad had ever got out of anything.

He ate the breakfast sitting on a fence in the bonnet, and while he ate it he began to feel a good deal better about himself, and by the end of it he had arranged the whole story so that it came out well.

He walked the last eight miles telling it to himself.

At the top of the hill above the river he stopped.

Below him were the willows, and the boathouse, and the roof of Toad Hall, and something else: on the drive in front of his own house there were two figures walking up and down with sticks, and there was washing hanging on the terrace where no washing had ever hung, and a window on the first floor was broken and stuffed with a sack.

Toad sat down in the grass in the washerwoman's gown and looked at his own house for a long time.

Then he went down the hill by the field path, keeping to the hedge, and came to the Rat's door and knocked.

The Rat opened it.

"Go away, my good woman. We do not want any washing done today."

"Ratty," said Toad. "It is me."

And the Rat took him in by both paws and pulled him into the hall, and looked at the gown and the bonnet, and said:

"Toad. Take those things off. — Have you had anything to eat since the day before yesterday?"

"No."

"Then eat first," said the Rat, "and I shall tell you about Toad Hall afterwards, and you are not going to like it, so eat first."

### Bölüm 11: Toad Hall Taken

Toad ate a great deal, and then the Rat told him.

On the night after the trial the weasels and the stoats and the ferrets from the Wild Wood had come out of the trees and down to Toad Hall, and had found the house with nobody in it but the Mole and the Badger, who had gone up to keep an eye on it; and there had been a fight in the dark on the lawn, and there were a great many of them and two of the others, and by morning the animals from the Wild Wood were inside the house with the doors barred.

They had been in it ever since. They slept in the beds. They had eaten most of what was in the cellar. They kept a sentry on the drive by day and two on the wall at night, and they had told the Mole, over the wall, in the politest way, that they were expecting Toad any day and would be glad to see him.

Toad stood up.

"I shall go up there and—"

"Sit down," said the Rat.

"It is my house."

"It is, and there are forty of them in it with guns, and you weigh as much as two of them and are worth about half of one in a fight. Sit down."

Toad sat down, and then got up again twice in the next hour and was made to sit down both times.

In the evening the Mole came in, very pleased with himself, having spent the afternoon walking up and down the towpath in the washerwoman's gown telling the sentries that a hundred armed animals were coming from three directions, which had cost the sentries their supper and most of their night.

And at ten o'clock the Badger came, and shook the water off his coat, and sat down in the big chair, and said:

"There is a passage."

"A what?"

"An underground passage," said the Badger, "that goes from the river bank up under the house and comes out in the butler's pantry, next to the banqueting hall. Your father built it. He showed it to me when you were a young toad, and he said to me, — I am telling you what he said, — he said: 'Do not tell my son about this passage. He is a good boy but he cannot keep his mouth shut. If he is ever in trouble, you may show him.'"

Toad opened his mouth to be offended.

Then he shut it again, which was, in the whole of that year, the most surprising thing he did.

"Tomorrow night," said the Badger, "there is a party in the banqueting hall. It is the Chief Weasel's birthday and they are all going to be in one room with no weapons — they leave them in the umbrella stand; the Mole heard it from the sentry — and they will be singing. We go up the passage, come out in the pantry, and go in at them from the inside."

"Four of us," said the Mole, "against forty."

"Four of us," said the Badger, "in the dark, from a door they are not watching, when they have had their supper. — Ratty, you will bring the sticks. Mole, you and I will go first. Toad, you will come last and you will not say anything at all until we are inside."

"I shall say—"

"You will not," said the Badger.

They spent the next day getting the sticks together and sharpening nothing, because the Badger said sticks would do, and there was a good deal of the Rat's arranging of belts and pistols which the Badger looked at once and did not comment on.

And at eleven o'clock the following night the four of them went down to the river bank in the dark and the Badger put his paw into a hole under the roots of a willow that Toad had climbed on a hundred times.

### Bölüm 12: The Tunnel

The passage was low and wet and it smelled of the river.

They went along it in single file with one lantern, and Toad, who was last, fell into a hole full of water almost at once and was pulled out and told that if he did that again he would be sent home.

After about ten minutes they began to hear something above them: a floor being stamped on, and a great many voices, and one voice going on longer than the others.

They came up under it. There was a trap door over their heads, and the Badger put his shoulder against it and lifted it an inch, and the noise came down at them.

Somebody in the hall was making a speech about Toad. There was a song about him, and the animals in the hall were joining in the chorus, and the song had four verses and got worse.

Toad, on the ladder in the dark, had to be held by the coat.

The Badger waited until the second chorus. Then he came up through the trap door into the pantry, and the other three after him, and they took their sticks in both paws, and the Badger opened the pantry door.

And then they went in.

The four of them came out of a door that nobody in that hall had thought about all evening, into the middle of forty animals who had put their guns in the umbrella stand at the other end of the room; and the Badger went straight up the middle of the hall and the Rat went along the left and the Mole went along the right, and Toad came in last, and — this is the only part of it he told afterwards, and he told it a great many times — the Chief Weasel went out of the window backwards over the table without anybody touching him at all.

It took about five minutes and there is nothing else in it worth writing down.

What is worth writing down is what the four of them found when it was over: a hall with the chairs broken and food on the floor and every window open, and forty animals gone out through the windows and across the lawn into the Wild Wood, and the house, which was very dirty, standing empty around them.

The Badger sat down on a bench and asked whether there was anything to eat.

They found cold chicken and a good deal of bread in the pantry and had it at one end of the long table at midnight, sitting on the two chairs that were left and a box, and Toad talked for most of it.

He was arranging a banquet by the time the chicken was finished. There were to be speeches. There was to be a song. There was to be a programme with his own name on it several times, and an address from himself on the subject of his adventures, with lantern slides if these could be got in time.

The Badger listened to about four minutes of it.

"No speeches," he said. "No song. You will send out the invitations, and you will stand at the door and shake hands and say nothing about yourself, and when they ask you about the prison and the barge you will say it was nothing much."

"Nothing much," said Toad.

"Nothing much."

"But it was a good deal," said Toad. "I got out of a prison in a bonnet. Nobody in this county has ever—"

The Badger looked at him.

Toad got up and went to the window and stood there for a while with his back to the room, and the other three went on eating and let him alone.

The banquet was held a fortnight later. Toad stood at the door and shook hands and said, four separate times, that it was nothing much, and each time he said it the Rat, standing behind him with the list of names, made a small noise that Toad chose not to hear.

Afterwards, when the guests had gone, the four of them went out onto the lawn.

It was the end of the summer and the river was low and quiet, and somewhere down the bank a moorhen was talking to itself.

"I shall write four letters tomorrow," said Toad. "To the gaoler's daughter, and to the engine-driver, and to the bargewoman for the horse, and there is a gipsy in a lane who was owed something."

"That is a great deal of writing," said the Rat.

"It is," said Toad, and went in to look for the paper.

The other three stayed out on the grass. Nobody said anything for some minutes, and then the Mole said that the willows wanted cutting back before the winter, and the Badger said that they did, and the Rat said nothing at all, and the three of them stood there and listened to the water going past in the dark.

## The Jungle Book

### Bölüm 1: The Cub in the Cave

The evening began the way every evening began. The wolf woke up in the mouth of the cave, scratched himself, and looked at his four cubs rolling in the dust.

Then a thin grey animal came up the path with his tail down. It was Tabaqui the jackal, who never hunted for himself and always ate what other animals left.

Mother Wolf did not move from the cubs.

"There is nothing here," she said.

"There is enough for a jackal," Tabaqui said. He licked a bone and put it down again. "I have news, and news costs nothing. Shere Khan is hunting in these hills now."

"He cannot," the father said. "This is not his ground."

"He has changed his ground," Tabaqui said. "And tonight he hunts man."

Nobody in the cave spoke for a moment. The Law of the Jungle allows hunting for food, and it does not allow hunting man, and it does not allow it for a reason: when one animal kills a man, men come back. They come back with lights and with guns, and they burn the grass, and everything that lives in the grass pays.

Tabaqui went away pleased.

Down in the valley a big animal roared, and then the roar broke and turned into a noise like a cat with a hurt paw.

"He has jumped at a woodcutters' fire," the father said. "He has burned his feet."

The dry grass on the slope moved. Something small pushed through it, held onto a low branch, let go, and stood up.

It was a baby. It was brown and naked and could just walk, and it looked into the cave with the fire of the valley behind it, and then it laughed and came in.

Mother Wolf put out one paw and pulled it in among her cubs, and it lay down there and went on drinking with the others as if it had always been there.

A shadow filled the mouth of the cave. It filled it completely, because a tiger's shoulders are wider than a wolf's cave.

"The cub came in here," Shere Khan said. "Give him to me."

"The cubs in this cave are mine," Mother Wolf said. "The man's cub is mine too."

"I do not talk to wolves. I take what runs into a wolf's house."

"You cannot come in," she said, and she did not stand up, and she did not raise her voice. "You have burned feet and a small door in front of you. Go back to the fire you jumped at and tell it what you take."

The shadow stayed. Then it moved back, and from further away the tiger said that the Pack would say what happened to a man's cub, and that a pack was easier to talk to than a mother.

The valley went quiet again.

Mother Wolf pushed the baby down between two of her own cubs. He had no hair anywhere, and she called him Mowgli, which in that jungle meant frog.

In the morning he was still there, and one of the cubs had put its head on his leg.

### Bölüm 2: The Council Rock

Once a month the wolves of that valley met on a flat rock, and every new cub was brought there to be looked at.

The Law says why. A cub who has been seen by the whole Pack can be recognised afterwards, anywhere, by any wolf. A cub nobody has seen belongs to nobody.

Akela led the Pack. He was grey almost to white, and he lay alone on the top of the rock, and forty wolves lay below him.

The cubs were pushed out into the open one by one. The mothers watched. The wolves looked, and said the two words the Law asks for, and the cubs went back.

Then Mother Wolf brought out the boy.

He sat down in the middle of the rock in the moonlight and did what a small child does. He picked up little stones and looked at them and put them in a line.

From behind the rocks a voice came, and everybody knew it.

"The cub is mine. Send him to me."

Akela did not turn his head. "Who spoke? This is the Pack's rock."

Some of the young wolves stood up. Some of them had eaten meat that the tiger had left for them, in the months when hunting was thin, and they said the same thing the tiger said: what does a wolf pack want with a man's cub?

The Law has one door for a cub in trouble. Two who are not wolves may speak for him, and if they speak, the Pack must listen.

The first was Baloo, the brown bear who teaches the cubs the Law. He came up slowly, because he was heavy and because he was old, and he said that he had taught every wolf on that rock, and that he would teach this one too, and that the boy could be sent away later if the teaching failed.

The second came without a sound, which is how a black panther comes.

"The Law also says a life may be bought," Bagheera said. "Nobody has to keep a cub for nothing. I have killed a bull, and it is lying at the bottom of the rock, and I have not touched it. Take the bull and let the cub live."

The wolves went down to look, because forty wolves will always go and look at a bull.

They ate it, and they came back up, and Akela said the thing that closes a council: the Pack had seen the cub, and the price was paid, and after this night nobody could say that he had come in by the back way.

"Look at him well," Akela said. "You have all eaten from the bull. Say it once more, so that nobody forgets who paid."

"Man's cub," said the Pack.

Down in the dark the tiger's voice said that the boy had years to live, and that years pass.

Baloo walked the boy off the rock himself. He was already talking, and the boy was not listening yet, and the lesson had begun anyway.

Mowgli went to sleep that night with the taste of bull on his hands, which was the first thing he owned in the jungle, and he had not paid for it.

### Bölüm 3: The Master Words

Baloo taught the boy for years, in the hot part of the day, on the same flat stone.

A wolf cub learns less than a man's cub, because a wolf only needs the Law of his own people. Mowgli had to learn all of it: which branch holds and which branch is rotten, what to say to bees, when to leave a snake alone, and how to speak to every people of the jungle.

That last part was the hardest and the most useful.

There is one sentence for each people — the hunting people, the birds, the snake people — and it means the same thing in all of them: *we are of one blood, you and I.* An animal who hears his own sentence will not touch you, and often will help you.

Mowgli learned them slowly and badly. When he got them wrong, the bear cuffed him, and the cuff of a bear who is being careful is still a cuff.

One afternoon the black panther came to see how the teaching went.

"Say the words of the birds," Bagheera said.

The boy said them, in the way a bird says them, and high up in a tree something answered him.

"Say the words of the snake people."

He said those too. There was a hiss behind the stone, and then nothing, and the boy looked pleased with himself in a way that no wolf ever looks.

"He is worth teaching," Bagheera said. "But a cub who knows he is clever is a cub who walks into the wrong branches."

"I have the Master Words of all the jungle," Mowgli said. "So why should I be afraid of anybody?"

Something came out of the tree above them and hit him on the head. It was a nutshell. Then more of them came down, and there was laughing in the leaves, and grey arms hung down and pulled back.

"The Monkey People," Baloo said, and he did not look up. "Do not use the Master Words on them."

"Why not? They have hands. They can pull thorns out. I would like a people with hands."

"They have no leader," the bear said. "They have no Law and no words of their own, and they say they are a great people, and they say it every day, because nobody else says it. They begin twenty things before the sun is high and they finish none of them. We do not go up there, and they do not come down here, and that is the whole of it."

"They have watched me all summer," Mowgli said.

"Then you have been watched by the only people in this jungle who forget what they have seen," Bagheera said.

That evening the two of them took him home along the river, one on each side, which was not usually necessary.

Before he slept, Mowgli said the words of the snake people into the dark, to hear how they sounded when nobody was testing him.

Something up in the branches said them back to him, wrong, and laughed.

### Bölüm 4: Carried Off

He slept between the bear and the panther at noon, the way he always did, and he woke up in the air.

Hands had him. Two of the Bandar-log had taken him by the arms, one on each side, and they went up through the branches with him and then out, over the tops of the trees, in long swings.

Below him he could hear the bear shouting, and it got smaller very quickly.

Nobody who lives on the ground ever sees the jungle from above. Mowgli saw it: the green top going on and on like water, the branches coming at his face, the ground appearing in holes and going away again. His arms hurt where the hands held him, and he did not call out, because he had already understood two things.

The first was that they would drop him if he fought.

The second was that the bear and the panther could not follow. Bears do not go along the top of the trees. Panthers cannot go where the branches are thin.

So he did the only useful thing there was to do. He looked up.

High above the trees a bird was turning in circles, the way that bird always turns in the middle of the day, looking down for something dead or something in trouble. It was Chil the kite, who sees everything in the jungle and says nothing to anybody unless he is spoken to properly.

Mowgli said the words of the birds.

The circle above him tightened. A kite came down two hundred feet in a straight line, and stayed with them for a moment, level with the treetops.

"You are a strange thing to find in the branches," Chil said.

"Mark my trail," Mowgli said. "Tell it to the brown bear and to the black panther at the council rock. Say it from me."

"For whose sake, brother?"

"For the Pack's, and mine. We are of one blood, you and I."

"So we are," the kite said, and he went up again, and then he went across.

The monkeys did not see any of it. Two of them were carrying him and shouting to the others about how strong they were, and the others were shouting back, and every one of them was busy being the most important animal in the trees.

They travelled all afternoon. When the sun went down they came over a hill, and Mowgli saw a grey thing below them in the jungle: walls, roofs with holes in them, straight streets with trees growing up through the stones. A city, empty for a very long time.

They dropped down into it, screaming, pleased with themselves.

"Now you have a people with hands," the biggest one said. "Say that we are the greatest people in the jungle. Say it, and we will teach you everything."

Mowgli sat where they had put him, on a cold stone floor, and rubbed his arms, and did not say it.

Somewhere over the hills, in the last light, a kite was going straight south with a message that nobody down here knew about.

### Bölüm 5: The Cold Lairs

The monkeys called the dead city the Cold Lairs, and they were sure it belonged to them.

They showed Mowgli everything that night. They showed him rooms with no roofs and a water tank with green water in it and a wall with a hole where something heavy had fallen out of it a hundred years ago.

Then they wanted things from him.

"You are a man's cub. Weave sticks together and make us a shelter for the rain."

He began to weave sticks, because he was interested in doing it. Twenty monkeys sat around him and watched, and then they got bored, and then they pulled the sticks apart to see how the ends were made, and by the time the moon was up nobody remembered that there had ever been a shelter.

That was when he understood what the bear had told him. It was not that the Monkey People were weak. There were hundreds of them and they had hands. It was that nothing they began was ever finished, so nothing they did could ever be counted on, not even a promise made ten minutes ago.

Late in the night two shapes came over the wall.

The bear came the slow way, along the ground. The panther came the fast way, over the stones, and went straight into the middle of them without stopping.

Monkeys are hard to fight, because there are always more of them behind you. Bagheera was in the water tank with fifty of them on him, and the bear had his back against a wall, and Mowgli was pushed into a small stone room with a broken roof and a door that four monkeys sat on.

The floor of the room moved.

There were snakes in it, old and thick and slow, and they had been there a long time, and they did not like the noise.

Mowgli said the words of the snake people, in the dark, without stopping.

"Ssso," said the floor, and lay still.

Outside, the noise changed all at once. It did not get louder. It stopped.

Something long was coming up the wall of the terrace: thirty feet of it, brown and dry, moving over the stones without any sound at all. Kaa the rock python did not hunt monkeys often, but the Bandar-log had once told him he was old and had lost his teeth, and a python remembers.

The monkeys went out of that city the way water goes out of a cracked pot.

They pulled Mowgli out of the stone room. He came out and looked at the panther in the tank and the bear against the wall and the great snake on the terrace, and he did what nobody had taught him to do.

He said thank you, and he said that if the python ever came to a trap with his own life inside it, then the boy's life was there to be used.

Kaa put his head down level with the boy's face and held it there for a while.

"A man's cub has thanked me," he said. "That has not happened before."

Then he went off to look for food, because he had come a long way and none of this had been his business.

The bear walked Mowgli home. Nobody said anything about the monkeys. On the way the boy stopped once, at the edge of the dead city, and looked back at the sticks he had woven, still lying pulled apart on the stones.

### Bölüm 6: The Red Flower

Years went by in that way, and the boy grew, and two things changed at the same time.

The first was that Akela got old. On the rock he still lay alone at the top, but he came back from hunting later than he used to.

The second was that the young wolves were fed. In the thin months the tiger left meat for them where they would find it, and a wolf who has eaten from the same kill twice will listen to whoever put it there.

The Law of the Pack has a hard line in it. The leader hunts in front of the Pack, and the day he misses his kill, the Pack may take his place from him. It is not cruelty. A pack that follows an animal who cannot hunt starves.

One evening Bagheera came to Mowgli at the river.

"Akela will miss soon," he said. "Then they will call for you on the rock, and the two who spoke for you have nothing left to say. The bull was paid once and it has been eaten."

"They know me," Mowgli said. "I have taken thorns out of their feet."

"They know you and they are afraid of you, and that is not the same as being one of them. Get the Red Flower before that night."

The Red Flower is what the jungle calls fire. No animal in that jungle will say the word, and no animal will stay near it.

So Mowgli went down to the village in the dark, on his stomach through the crop, up to a hut where a woman kept a small clay pot with coals in it and fed it dry cakes of dung all night long. He watched her do it for an hour. Then he took the pot, and carried it back up the hill, and fed it sticks all the way, and it did not go out.

Akela missed his kill four nights later. The young buck went past him and he was too slow, and the Pack saw it.

They came to the rock in a bad mood. The young wolves sat in front, and the voice from behind the stones was there again, closer than it used to be.

"He has missed. Give us the leader's place, and give us the man's cub."

Mowgli stood up in the middle of the rock with the clay pot in his hands, and he put a dry branch into it, and the branch caught, and he held it up.

Forty wolves went backwards. The voice behind the stones stopped in the middle of a sentence.

"You have called me a man all my life," Mowgli said. "Tonight you are right. Here is what men have."

He walked across the rock with the burning branch and nobody came near him, and he set the clay pot down on the stone beside Akela, where the leader had always lain.

"Akela hunts where he likes," he said, "and this stays here."

Then he said the rest of it, which was that he was going to the men, because the wolves had told him twenty times that he was one, and that there was one debt left in this jungle and it had a striped skin.

The wolves let him go down the hill. Nobody followed him, and nobody said the two words of the council either.

At the bottom he looked back once. The small light was still on the rock.

### Bölüm 7: The Village and the Herd

He came into the village in the middle of the afternoon and sat down inside the gate, because he did not know that a man is supposed to knock.

A woman took him into her house. She had lost a boy to a tiger years before and she looked at Mowgli for a long time and then gave him milk and bread, and after that she did not ask him where he had come from.

Living with men had to be learned like anything else.

He learned that a floor is for walking on and not for sleeping on, and that a cloth goes around you even when it is hot, and that money is small round metal which you give to a man to get rice, which he thought was a very strange way to hunt.

He learned the word for plough and the word for roof and the word for a coin.

In the evenings the men sat under the big tree by the gate and told stories about the jungle. The oldest one, who had been a hunter, said that tigers change bodies with dead men and that the eyes are how you know.

Mowgli sat with his back against the tree and looked at the ground and said nothing, because a boy who has taken thorns out of a wolf's foot cannot say anything useful in that conversation.

They gave him work. Every morning he took the village buffaloes out to graze, which is a boy's job, because buffaloes are slow and stupid and only one thing frightens them.

He liked it. He rode on the back of the biggest one all day, in the heat, at the edge of the jungle, where the grass was tall.

On the eleventh morning something grey came out of the grass and lay down beside the buffaloes as if it had always been there.

"You have a smell of smoke and cows now," Grey Brother said.

"I have a roof, and a name in a house, and I have counted the same animals eleven times," Mowgli said. "How is the Pack?"

"Split. Akela hunts alone and keeps his place because of the light on the rock, and nobody has taken it away, and nobody sits near it either."

"And the other one?"

"That is why I came. He has been away in the north, and he came back last night, and he is asleep in the dry valley up there, full of a kill, and he means to wait for you in it."

Mowgli sat on the buffalo's back and looked at the hill where the valley was. It had steep sides, and one narrow way in at the top, and one narrow way out at the bottom.

He counted the buffaloes again, and then he counted the bulls in them, and then the cows and the calves, and the two ends of the valley, and the whole of it fitted together in his head like the sticks he had woven in the dead city.

He put his hand on the wolf's head.

Then he took the herd home at the usual time, and counted them at the gate the way men do, and went in to eat his bread as if the day had had nothing in it.

### Bölüm 8: The Valley

He took the herd out before light the next morning, and he took it the wrong way, up the hill.

At the top he split it. The bulls went with him along the ridge to the upper end of the dry valley. The cows and the calves went to the bottom end with the wolf keeping them together, and cows with calves behind them do not go backwards for anything in the world.

Then he shouted down into the valley twice, and stopped, and waited.

A tiger who has eaten all night and slept all morning cannot climb a steep side quickly. Cattle can go down a slope faster than they can do anything else, and forty of them going down together do not stop and cannot be turned.

It was over in less time than it takes to weave a stick shelter. The dust came up out of the valley and hung there, and when it went down again the buffaloes were standing at the bottom, breathing, and the thing they had been driven at did not come out of the valley at all.

Mowgli came down and looked at it for a while.

"That is the whole of it, then," he said. "Twelve years, and a valley, and cows."

He worked all afternoon with his knife, and the wolf helped him, and by evening the skin was off and rolled up, and he tied it on the back of the biggest buffalo and took the herd home.

The village saw him coming with it before he reached the gate.

There was a lot of noise. The old hunter, who had promised the village a hundred coins for that skin for eleven years, said that the boy could not have done it. Then he said that the boy could only have done it with the jungle behind him. Then he said the word that men use in that part of the country for a boy who talks to animals, and stones started coming.

The woman came out of her house and stood in front of him for a moment and told him to run, and he saw that she believed the word too, a little, and would have kept him anyway.

He went out of the gate with the skin. Nobody followed him past the crop.

He walked all night and came up to the flat rock in the grey light. The clay pot was still on the stone, out, cold, half full of ash.

Mowgli spread the skin on the rock where the pot had been.

Akela came and lay down on it, because he was old and the stone was cold, and four grown wolves came and sat below the rock: the four cubs he had slept between when he could just walk.

The Pack did not come. Some of them came near, and looked, and went away again. Nobody said the two words.

"You are not of the village now," Grey Brother said.

"And not of the Pack," Mowgli said.

"Then what?"

He did not have a word for it, and neither did the jungle, and neither did the village.

He sat down on the edge of the rock with the four of them, and the sun came up, and below them the smoke of the village went up straight, and behind them the trees started making their morning noise.

### Bölüm 9: Rikki-tikki-tavi

The flood took him out of his own hole in the bank and carried him down a ditch, and when the water went down he was lying in the middle of a garden path, wet, with his eyes shut.

A family found him there. They had come to live in the white house at the end of the garden, and the small boy carried him in, and they dried him in front of a fire and gave him a piece of raw meat and a saucer of water.

He was a young mongoose. He was the colour of dust, with a pink nose and pink feet, and his tail went up when he was interested, which was most of the time.

By the second morning he had been everywhere in that house — into the bath, up onto the writing table, inside a lamp — because a mongoose wants to know all of a place and there is no other way to do it.

His name was Rikki-tikki-tavi, which was the noise he made in the grass.

Then he went out into the garden, and the garden was better than the house.

In a bush near the wall a bird was singing very loudly and very badly. It was Darzee the tailorbird, and he had built his nest out of two big leaves sewn together with grass, and his wife was sitting in it, and both of them were making a great deal of noise about nothing.

"We are unhappy," Darzee sang. "One of our young ones fell out of the nest yesterday, and Nag ate him."

"That is bad news," said Rikki-tikki. "But I am new here. Who is Nag?"

The grass by the wall moved and went on moving, and a very large black cobra came out of it, five feet of him, and lifted the front third of himself up off the ground, and looked down at the mongoose with two flat eyes.

"I am Nag," he said. "This garden was ours before the house was built. Look at me and be afraid."

Rikki-tikki knew, the way a young mongoose knows without being taught, what his family had always done for a living. He also knew that no mongoose can win against a cobra head-on when the cobra is ready.

So he did not look at the head.

He heard the grass behind him instead, and he jumped straight up, and the second cobra came through the place where he had been standing and hit nothing.

That was Nagaina, and she was bigger than her husband.

They both went back into the grass without another word, because a snake who has missed does not stay to talk.

Later that morning the small boy was walking down the path, and a little brown snake, the kind that lies in the dust and is easy not to see, was lying between the boy's foot and the next stone.

Rikki-tikki got there first. It was very quick, and afterwards there was nothing on the path but dust.

The family made a lot of noise about him and carried him about and called him a clever thing and a hero, and he ate his supper and did not understand any of it.

That night he did not sleep on the boy's bed. He walked round the house instead, because that is where a mongoose's work is.

### Bölüm 10: The Two Voices by the Wall

The house was quiet that night, and Rikki-tikki was awake by the bathroom wall when the two of them began to talk on the other side of it.

"When the big man is dead," Nagaina said, "the garden will be quiet again, and the mongoose will have to go, and our young ones will come out into an empty house."

"Are you sure the man must die?" Nag said.

"He brought the mongoose. Kill him, and the woman, and the boy, and then there is nobody."

"I will go in by the water drain," Nag said. "I will wait behind the big water jar until morning and kill him when he comes."

Rikki-tikki heard the wet sound of the cobra coming in through the pipe, and then five feet of him going across the bathroom floor, and then nothing at all, because a snake waiting behind a jar makes no noise for hours.

He waited too.

An hour before light he went in. He knew that if he took the tail he would be shaken off, and that the only place worth taking was the head, behind it, above the hood, and that once he had it he would have to keep it.

It was loud, and it went on for some time, and things fell over. Then the big man came in from the bedroom with a lamp and a long stick, and afterwards he carried something out into the garden on the end of it, and the woman had to be helped back to bed.

Rikki-tikki lay on the bathroom floor and shook, and then he shook himself properly and went out, because half the work was still in the garden.

Darzee was already sitting in his nest singing a song about how Nag was dead and the garden was safe, which was the sort of song Darzee was best at and the least useful thing anybody could have been doing.

"Where are the eggs?" said Rikki-tikki.

"In the melon bed, at the end nearest the wall," said Darzee's wife, who was more sensible. "She put them there twenty-five days ago."

He found them: twenty-five white eggs with soft skins, the size of a small hen's egg, with young cobras inside them ready to come out.

He dealt with twenty-four of them, quickly, in the hot sun.

Then Darzee's wife came screaming down the melon bed to say that Nagaina had gone up to the veranda, where the family were sitting at breakfast, and that she was between the small boy's chair and the wall.

Rikki-tikki came up the path with the last egg in his mouth and put it down on the stones where she could see it.

"Look at what is in front of me," he said. "Come and get it."

She came, and she left the boy, and that was the whole point.

He got the egg and she got herself into the rat hole under the thorn bush at the bottom of the garden, and she took the last egg with her, and Rikki-tikki went in after her, down into the dark, where no mongoose likes to go.

Up in the tree Darzee began, immediately, to sing a very fine song about how brave Rikki-tikki had been and how sad it was that he was dead.

He was still singing it when the earth at the mouth of the hole moved and a dusty grey animal pulled himself out backwards into the daylight, and lay in the sun, and did not move for a long time.

"Stop that song," he said. "And tell the coppersmith to give the news, so that the birds know the garden is empty."

He slept most of that day on the veranda, in the way of everybody's feet, and after that he walked round the house every night, which is what a mongoose does when a house is his ground.

## The Happy Prince

Kimlik 1110 · A2 · 1589 kelime · 9 dk

### Bölüm 1: The Statue Above the City

High above the city, on a tall column, stood the statue of the Happy Prince. He was covered, from head to foot, with thin leaves of fine gold. His eyes were two blue stones, bright as the sea. A large red stone shone on the handle of his sword.

The whole city was proud of him.

"He is very beautiful," said the Town Councillor, looking up. "And he never asks for anything. That is my favourite kind of art."

Mothers showed him to their crying children. "Look at the Happy Prince," they said. "The Happy Prince never cries."

One night a little swallow flew over the roofs. His friends had left for the warm south six weeks before. He had stayed behind, because he loved a reed that grew by the river. The reed was slim and beautiful, but she never said anything, and she never wanted to travel. So at last the swallow left her and flew away alone.

"Where can I sleep?" he said. "I hope the town has a place ready for me."

Then he saw the statue on the tall column.

"I will sleep there," he said. "It is a fine place, with plenty of fresh air." And he landed between the feet of the Happy Prince.

"I have a golden bedroom," he said softly, looking at the gold around him. He put his head under his wing. Just then a large drop of water fell on him.

"How strange!" he said. "There is not one cloud in the sky, and it is raining."

A second drop fell.

The swallow looked up.

The eyes of the golden Prince were wet, and two lines of water ran slowly down the golden face. In the moonlight the drops shone like the blue stones themselves.

The little bird sat very still and looked at the wet gold for a long time. Above the city, from one end of the sky to the other, there was not a single cloud.

### Bölüm 2: The Ruby

"Who are you?" asked the swallow.

"I am the Happy Prince."

"Then why is your face wet? You have made my feathers wet too."

"When I had a man's heart, I lived in a palace," said the statue. "There was a high wall around the garden. I never asked what was behind the wall. Everything near me was beautiful, so everyone called me the Happy Prince. Now I stand up here, higher than the wall, and I can see the whole city. My heart is made of lead now — but I can see."

The swallow said nothing. He waited.

"Far away," said the statue, "in a little street, there is a poor house. A woman sits at a table, sewing flowers on a fine dress for a rich lady. Her hands are red from work. In the corner, her little boy lies in bed. He is ill. He asks for oranges, and his mother has only river water to give him. Swallow, Swallow, little Swallow — will you take her the red stone from my sword? My feet are fixed to this column, and I cannot move."

"My friends are waiting for me in the south," said the swallow. "They fly over the great river all day and sleep in the warm sand."

"Swallow, Swallow, little Swallow," said the Prince, "stay with me for one night. Be my messenger. The boy is thirsty, and the mother is tired."

"I don't like boys very much," said the swallow. "Last summer, some boys by the river threw stones at me." He looked at the Prince's wet face. "But it is cold up here, and one night is only one night. I will do it."

He took the great red stone out of the sword and flew away over the roofs, with the stone in his beak.

The window of the poor house was open. The woman had fallen asleep over her sewing. The swallow flew in quietly and laid the red stone on the table, next to her needle. Then he flew softly around the bed and moved the air over the boy's face with his wings.

"It is cooler," said the boy. "I think I am getting better." And he fell into a sweet sleep.

The swallow flew back to the Prince. "It is strange," he said. "The night is very cold, but I feel quite warm now."

He began to think about it, and then he fell asleep. Thinking always made him sleepy.

### Bölüm 3: The Sapphire Eyes

The next evening the swallow flew up to the Prince. "Tonight I fly to the south," he said. "It is my last night here."

"Swallow, Swallow, little Swallow," said the Prince, "far across the city, in a small cold room under the roof, a young man sits at a table full of papers. He is writing a play for the theatre. But there is no fire in his room and no bread in his cupboard, and his hands are too cold to write."

"I will wait one more night," said the swallow. "Shall I take him another red stone?"

"I have no red stone now," said the Prince. "Only my eyes are left. They are two rare blue stones, brought long ago from over the sea. Take one to him. He will sell it, buy wood and bread, and finish his play."

"Dear Prince," said the swallow, "I cannot do that." And he turned his head away.

"Swallow, Swallow, little Swallow," said the Prince, "do as I ask."

So the swallow took out one of the Prince's eyes and flew to the room under the roof. There was a hole in the roof, and he went in through it. The young man sat with his head in his hands and did not hear the wings. When he looked up, a beautiful blue stone lay on the dried flowers on his table.

"Somebody knows about my play," he said. He held the stone up to the light. Then he pulled his chair to the table and took up his pen.

The next evening the swallow came back. "I came to say goodbye," he said.

"Swallow, Swallow, little Swallow," said the Prince, "in the square below there is a little match-girl. Her matches have fallen into the water, and now nobody will buy them. She has no shoes, and she is afraid to go home with empty hands. Take her my other eye."

"Then you will be blind," said the swallow.

"Swallow, Swallow, little Swallow, do as I ask."

So the swallow took out the Prince's other eye and flew down with it. He passed the match-girl like a shadow and dropped the stone into her hand.

"What a pretty piece of glass!" cried the little girl, and she ran home, laughing.

The swallow flew back to the Prince. "You are blind now," he said, "so I will stay with you always."

"No, little Swallow. You must go away."

"I will stay with you always," said the swallow.

Where the sea-blue stones had been, the golden face now had two dark, quiet places. The swallow slept between the Prince's feet.

### Bölüm 4: The Lead Heart

The swallow stayed with the Prince. He was the Prince's eyes now. All day he flew over the city, and in the evening he told the Prince what he had seen: children with no bread, people with no fire, rich doors that stayed closed.

"I am covered with fine gold," said the Prince. "Take it off, leaf by leaf, and give it to the people who need it."

Leaf by leaf, the swallow carried the gold away. Leaf by leaf, the statue grew grey and dull. And in the little streets, children's faces grew rounder, and there was bread on the tables.

Then the snow came, and after the snow the frost. The streets shone like silver. Everyone wore warm coats. The little swallow grew colder and colder, but he did not leave the Prince. He picked up small pieces of bread outside the baker's door and tried to keep warm by moving his wings.

At last he knew that only a little time was left.

"Goodbye, dear Prince," he said.

"I am glad you are going south at last, little Swallow," said the Prince. "You have stayed too long."

"I am not going south," said the swallow. He kissed the Prince, and fell down dead at his feet.

At that moment a strange sound came from inside the statue — a small, dry crack, like something breaking. The lead heart had broken in two. That night, the frost was very hard.

Early the next morning, the Mayor walked across the square with the Town Councillors. He looked up at the column.

"How grey the Happy Prince looks!" he said. "The red stone is gone. The eyes are gone. The gold is gone. He is no better than a beggar. And there is a dead bird at his feet! Since he is no longer beautiful, he is no longer useful."

So they pulled the statue down and put it into a great furnace, to melt the metal for a new statue.

But the broken lead heart would not melt. The workmen took it out and threw it away, onto the dust-heap behind the factory — where the dead swallow was also lying.

The heart and the bird lay side by side in the grey dust.

"Bring me the two most precious things in the city," God said to one of his angels.

The angel went down, and came back with the lead heart and the dead bird.

## The Empty Chair

*The same morning, 7:40. Howard's kitchen.*

Howard put his coat on at ten to eight. He always put it on at ten to eight.

On the table there was a blue mug with a little ship on it. The mug was empty. It was always empty. Nobody had made coffee in that mug for four years.

He walked to the door. He put his hand on the door. Then he looked at the clock.

Two minutes past eight.

Howard stood there for a moment. Then he took his coat off again and hung it up. He sat down at the table, in front of the empty blue mug. He did not turn the radio on.

---

The other blue mug stood ready on the counter. It was 8:15. Howard's chair was empty.

Emma looked at the door. Howard came every morning at 8:15. Same time, same table, same blue mug with the little ship on it. Emma never needed to ask. She just made his coffee: black, one sugar.

Rosa came from the kitchen with a tray of croissants. "Why are you standing there?"

"It's 8:20," Emma said.

Rosa looked at the empty chair by the window. She put the tray down slowly. "Maybe he's sleeping."

"Howard doesn't sleep late."

"Maybe the bus was late."

"He walks."

"Then something has happened," Rosa said.

"Nothing has happened. It's 8:20."

"You said that already." Rosa put the croissants in the glass case, one by one. "Twice."

On the window table there were two coins. Howard left two coins next to his mug every morning. Emma picked them up and put them in the little dish by the till. Then she looked at the door again.

A young woman came in and ordered a cappuccino. Then a man with a laptop. The shop got busy. Emma made coffee, took money, smiled. But every time the door opened, she looked up fast.

At nine, an old man came in. It wasn't Howard. He bought a tea and left. Emma watched him all the way down the street.

Rosa came to the counter. "You can call him, you know."

"I don't have his number."

"You have made his coffee every day for three years. And you don't have his number?"

Emma looked at the blue mug. The coffee in it was cold now. "He's a customer, Rosa."

"Is he? Say it again, then."

"He's a customer."

"Hmm," Rosa said, and went back to the kitchen.

Emma poured the cold coffee away. It was the first coffee she had thrown away in three years.

Behind the counter there was a small photo on the wall: the shop's first day. In the photo, Howard stood next to the door with flowers in his hand. He was the first customer. He was here before Emma.

At twelve, the lunch people came. At three, the shop was quiet again. The blue mug stayed by the till.

At closing time, Emma cleaned the tables. At the window table, she stopped. Under Howard's chair there was something small and dark. She picked it up.

It was a glasses case. Old, brown leather. There were no glasses inside. Only a piece of paper, folded two times.

Emma stood with the paper in her hand. It wasn't hers. It was private.

She looked at the door. Then at the paper again.

"Rosa," she called. "What time do we open tomorrow?"

Rosa's head appeared at the kitchen door. "Eight. Same as every day. Why?"

Emma put the glasses case under the counter, next to the blue mug and the two coins.

"No reason," she said. "Just asking."

## Black, One Sugar

Kimlik 818 · A2 · 590 kelime · 5 dk · dizi: Coffee Shop Stories · sezon 1 · bölüm 2

*The same day, 6:40. The kitchen.*

Rosa opened the cafe at twenty to seven, like every morning. She put the radio on low. She made two trays of dough.

At seven her phone made a small sound on the metal table.

Rosa looked at it. She did not pick it up. She turned it face down and went back to the dough. Her hands worked faster for a minute or two.
